Lecture by lecture

Each lecture's own References section, as printed at its end.

Lecture 0 · Why integrability?

M. Toda, Theory of Nonlinear Lattices, §1.1 (the FPU problem, its parameters and the recurrence), §2.7 and §2.10; E. Fermi, J. Pasta and S. Ulam, Los Alamos report LA-1940 (1955); T. Dauxois, Physics Today 61(1) (2008) 55; N. J. Zabusky and M. D. Kruskal, Phys. Rev. Lett. 15 (1965) 240; M. Toda, J. Phys. Soc. Japan 22 (1967) 431; G. Benettin, H. Christodoulidi and A. Ponno, J. Stat. Phys. 152 (2013) 195; T. Kinoshita, T. Wenger and D. S. Weiss, Nature 440 (2006) 900; J.-S. Caux and J. Mossel, J. Stat. Mech. (2011) P02023.

Lecture 1 · Phase space and flows

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, Ch. 8 (vector fields and their flows on a manifold); da Silva, Lectures on Symplectic Geometry, §§1–3 (manifolds and vector fields); Babelon, Bernard and Talon, Introduction to Classical Integrable Systems, Ch. 1.

Lecture 2 · Two languages: the Poisson bracket and differential forms

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, Ch. 8 (Hamiltonian vector fields, the Lie algebra of Hamiltonian functions) and §32 (exterior algebra); da Silva, Lectures on Symplectic Geometry, §§1–3 and 18 (Poisson structures) and §6 (forms); Babelon, Bernard and Talon, Introduction to Classical Integrable Systems, Ch. 1. Sign conventions differ across all three sources for precisely the identity of §2.3: check any formula against (2.7) and (2.6) before transcribing it.

Lecture 3 · The exterior derivative, Stokes, and the symplectic form

da Silva, Lectures on Symplectic Geometry, §1 (symplectic forms) and §6 (forms, d\ddd, the Poincaré lemma, Stokes); Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §§35–36 (integration of forms, Stokes), §37 (symplectic structures) and §44 (the action integral this lecture was built to support).

Lecture 4 · One structure: canonical maps and Lagrangian submanifolds

da Silva, Lectures on Symplectic Geometry, §§1–4 (symplectic forms, cotangent bundles and the tautological form, Lagrangian submanifolds, generating functions) and §§7–8 (the Moser trick and Darboux); Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §§37–38 and 40–41 (symplectic structures, integral invariants, the Poisson bracket from ω\sympω), §43 (Darboux), §44 (the integral invariant of Poincaré–Cartan) and §§47–48 (Hamilton–Jacobi, generating functions); Bolsinov and Fomenko, Integrable Hamiltonian Systems, §1.3 (Darboux, and its version adapted to commuting functions). Conventions. da Silva’s agree with ours exactly — ω=−dα\symp=-\dd\alphaω=−dα with α\alphaα the tautological form, ιXHω=dH\iota_{\hvf{H}}\symp=\dd HιXH​​ω=dH, {f,g}=ω(Xf,Xg)\pb{f}{g}=\symp(\hvf{f},\hvf{g}){f,g}=ω(Xf​,Xg​), and the engine’s minus sign. Arnold and Bolsinov–Fomenko write ω=∑dp∧dq\symp=\sum\dd p\wedge\dd qω=∑dp∧dq, so their ω\sympω is minus ours: check any formula from them against (3.13) and Theorem 4.17 before transcribing it.

Lecture 5 · The Liouville–Arnold theorem

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §49 (the theorem and its proof; Lemma 2 of §49 C is our stage (iii), Lemma 3 of §49 D our Lemma 5.10); Babelon, Bernard and Talon, Introduction to Classical Integrable Systems, ch. 2 (the theorem, and the examples). Conventions. Arnold writes ω=∑dp∧dq\symp=\sum\dd p\wedge\dd qω=∑dp∧dq, the negative of ours; nothing in this lecture depends on the sign of ω\sympω, since every use of it is a statement that something vanishes.

Lecture 6 · Action–angle variables

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §50 (action–angle variables: the construction and the neighbourhood statement), §51 (conditionally periodic motion) and §8 (central fields, Bertrand’s theorem); Babelon, Bernard and Talon, Introduction to Classical Integrable Systems, ch. 2; DLMF, §§19.2 and 19.12 (complete elliptic integrals and their behaviour as k→1k\to1k→1). Conventions. Arnold writes ω=∑dp∧dq\symp=\sum\dd p\wedge\dd qω=∑dp∧dq, the negative of ours; his actions come out positive for the same reason ours do (§3.4).

Lecture 7 · When integrability fails

Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, App. 8–9; Kozlov, Russian Math. Surveys 38:1 (1983), ch. IV §1; Ott, Chaos in Dynamical Systems, §§4.4, 7.2–7.3; Toda, Theory of Nonlinear Lattices, §§1.2, 2.9; Hénon and Heiles, Astron. J. 69 (1964) 73; Ito (1985) and Morales-Ruiz, Ramis and Simó (2007) for the Hénon–Heiles family.

Lecture 8 · Lax pairs, and the spectral parameter

Babelon, Bernard and Talon, Introduction to Classical Integrable Systems, ch. 3 (Lax pairs) and ch. 2 on Toda; Toda, Theory of Nonlinear Lattices, §§2.10–2.11 (Flaschka’s variables and the periodic chain); H. Flaschka, Phys. Rev. B 9 (1974) 1924; M. Hénon, Phys. Rev. B 9 (1974) 1921; M. Audin, Spinning Tops (the Euler top, in the reading).

Lecture 9 · Why the integrals commute: the Toda ring is integrable

Babelon, Bernard and Talon, Introduction to Classical Integrable Systems, ch. 2–3 (the Sklyanin bracket) and ch. 6 (the periodic chain, its 2×22\times22×2 monodromy matrix and its spectral curve); Faddeev and Takhtajan, Hamiltonian Methods in the Theory of Solitons, Part I ch. 1 (the classical r\rmatr-matrix and the transition to the quantum case); H. Flaschka and D. W. McLaughlin, Prog. Theor. Phys. 55 (1976) 438 (the actions from the gaps). Conventions. Babelon–Bernard–Talon exchange the letters λ\lambdaλ and zzz, and conventions for the sign of r\rmatr differ between all three; see the third caveat.
