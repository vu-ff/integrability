Lecture 6

When integrability fails

What this lecture does

Lectures 4 and 5 built the integrable world: tori, actions, frequencies. This lecture perturbs it. Poincaré’s theorem says that a perturbation, under conditions we state exactly, destroys the integrals; the KAM theorem says that most of the tori survive anyway; between the two, the resonant tori break into island chains and chaos. We watch all of it happen in the Hénon–Heiles system, put it beside the Toda chain it truncates, and end with the precise sense — and the only sense this course claims — in which integrability is exceptional.

6.3 The KAM theorem

Poincaré’s theorem destroys the integrals, but not necessarily the tori. A torus is an invariant set, and it may survive even where no integral does; Proposition 6.4 cannot build it, because of the resonances. The tori to hope for are those furthest from every resonance.

That is Arnold’s statement (App. 8 B, Kolmogorov’s theorem, and the hypotheses on the following page); the last sentence is from his sketch of the proof, which keeps the tori with ∣k⋅ν∣≥C∣k∣−τ\abs{k\cdot\freq}\ge C\abs k^{-\tau}∣k⋅ν∣≥C∣k∣−τ and discards the rest — a set of small measure when CCC is small, as Lemma 6.7 shows. Three things the theorem does not say. It gives no size for “sufficiently small” and no rate for “small”. It says nothing about the resonant tori: that is the next section. And it gives no integral: the surviving tori are indexed by Diophantine frequencies, a set with no interior, and leave gaps round every resonance, which by Theorem 6.3 no integral that is a power series in ε\varepsilonε fills in. That is how the two theorems are both true. The proof builds each surviving torus by Newton’s method rather than by the series (6.4), and uses Definition 6.6 at every step to control the denominators; we do not give it.

For n=2n=2n=2 it has a sharp consequence. On a three-dimensional energy level a two-dimensional torus separates, like a surface of revolution in space: an orbit starting between two surviving tori stays between them forever, and its actions stay near their initial values (Arnold, App. 8, p. 407, under the analogous isoenergetic non-degeneracy condition). For n≥3n\ge3n≥3 the tori separate nothing, and a slow drift through the gaps is possible — Arnold diffusion, which we only name.

Theorem 6.10 (Poincaré’s geometric theorem cited). An area-preserving homeomorphism of a closed annulus to itself that turns its two boundary circles in opposite directions has at least two fixed points.

Reading

Two steps hide in “the inner boundary turns back”. Arnold’s hypothesis is pointwise: every point of the inner circle must move backwards. That holds because a circle homeomorphism with rotation number ρ−<a/b\rho_-<a/bρ−​<a/b moves every point by less than 2πa2\pi a2πa in bbb steps (if some point moved by exactly 2πa2\pi a2πa the rotation number would be a/ba/ba/b). And the two fixed points of the theorem might lie on one orbit; that there are two distinct periodic orbits coming from this resonant torus is Arnold’s corollary (p. 422): when an nnn-dimensional torus filled with closed trajectories of an isoenergetically non-degenerate system breaks up under a sufficiently small perturbation, at least 2n−12^{n-1}2n−1 closed trajectories form, counted with multiplicity, of which at least nnn are geometrically distinct.

In the generic case there are kbkbkb periodic points of each of two kinds, and they alternate round the old circle (Ott, §7.2.2): elliptic points, surrounded by small invariant curves of their own, and hyperbolic points, saddles of Pε bP_\varepsilon^{\,b}Pεb​. The resonant torus has become an island chain (Figure 6.4).

The hyperbolic points carry the chaos. A hyperbolic fixed point has a stable curve WsW^{s}Ws of points that approach it, and an unstable curve WuW^{u}Wu of points that approach it backwards in time. In an integrable system they join smoothly into a separatrix, as for the pendulum (§5.3). Perturbed, they generically cross. One crossing xxx forces infinitely many, since every image PmxP^{m}xPmx is again on both curves; the lobes between successive crossings have equal areas because the map preserves area, and as they crowd towards the fixed point they must stretch into long thin tongues. This homoclinic tangle (Figure 6.6), discovered by Poincaré, forces chaotic dynamics nearby (Ott, §§4.3, 7.2.2), and it is where the chaotic sea of Figure 6.5 is born.

6.5 Poincaré sections: Hénon–Heiles

Hénon and Heiles wanted to know whether a star in an axisymmetric galaxy has a third integral besides the energy and the angular momentum. The question reduces to two degrees of freedom, and they took as the simplest model H=12(px2+py2)+12(x2+y2)+x2y−13y3. How to make a section

Take Σ={x=0, px>0}\Sigma=\{x=0,\ p_x>0\}Σ={x=0, px​>0} at fixed EEE, with coordinates (y,py)(y,p_y)(y,py​); the allowed region is 12py2+12y2−13y3≤E\tfrac12p_y^{2}+\tfrac12y^{2}-\tfrac13y^{3}\le E21​py2​+21​y2−31​y3≤E.   • an orbit on an invariant torus fills a smooth closed curve;

  • an orbit in an island chain fills several small curves, visited in turn;

  • a chaotic orbit fills a region, erratically, and its points scatter.

Figure 6.7 is the result, and it is what Hénon and Heiles saw in 1964. At low energy the section is foliated by curves: every orbit looks as if it had a second integral. That resembles the picture Theorem 6.8 gives for non-degenerate systems as ε→0\varepsilon\to0ε→0, but it is not a prediction of the theorem, which does not cover this degenerate case; it is a measurement.

6.6 Toda against Hénon–Heiles at equal energy

Three particles on a ring, each pushed by its neighbours through the exponential potential of Toda, with the centre of mass removed and the coordinates, time and energy rescaled (Toda, §2.9), have the Hamiltonian HToda=12(px2+py2)+124(e2y+23x+e2y−23x+e−4y)−18. The integrable one is not the “simplified” one: dropping terms made the problem harder, not easier. Integrability is a property of the whole Hamiltonian: this example shows that the cubic Taylor polynomial cannot see it, and that it need not survive truncation. That is why this course spends its effort on the exceptional systems exactly as they are, and treats the rest of the world as a perturbation of them — the argument of §0.5, now with a picture.

6.7 Lyapunov exponents, and genericity stated precisely

The eye separates curves from scatter; a number does it better.

Definition 6.11 (Lyapunov exponent). For an orbit Φt(x0)\Phi_t(x_0)Φt​(x0​) and a tangent vector uuu at x0x_0x0​, λ(x0,u)=lim⁡t→∞1tln⁡∣dΦt(u)∣∣u∣,\lambda(x_0,u)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac1t\ln\frac{\abs{\dd\Phi_t(u)}}{\abs u},λ(x0​,u)=t→∞lim​t1​ln∣u∣∣dΦt​(u)∣​, where it exists (Ott, §4.4). A positive exponent alone is not chaos: the hyperbolic fixed point of the standard map has one. For a bounded orbit that is not periodic, λ>0\lambda>0λ>0 is the standard numerical indicator of chaos, and that is how it is used here.

On a torus of an integrable system, in action–angle coordinates φ(t)=φ(0)+ν(I)t\varphi(t)=\varphi(0)+\freq(I)tφ(t)=φ(0)+ν(I)t, so a small change δI\delta IδI gives δφ(t)=δφ(0)+t (∂ν/∂I) δI\delta\varphi(t)=\delta\varphi(0)+t\,(\partial\freq/\partial I)\,\delta Iδφ(t)=δφ(0)+t(∂ν/∂I)δI: nearby orbits separate linearly, by shear, and every λ\lambdaλ is 000 (in any coordinates: near a compact torus all norms are comparable). In a chaotic region they separate exponentially (Figure 6.10). The limit is never reached numerically; what is computed is the finite-time exponent, which is how Figure 6.8 was made.

Finally, genericity. The course has claimed that integrability is the exception. Here is exactly what it can support.

  1. In the perturbation problem (6.1), with HHH real-analytic in (φ,I,ε)(\varphi,I,\varepsilon)(φ,I,ε), H0H_0H0​ non-degenerate and the Poincaré set dense, there is no second integral, independent of HHH, that is a power series in ε\varepsilonε with smooth coefficients (Theorem 6.3). What we proved (Proposition 6.5) is its first step: the leading term of any such integral is a function of H0H_0H0​.

  2. In the Hénon–Heiles family Vλ=12(x2+y2)+x2y+λ3y3V_\lambda=\tfrac12(x^{2}+y^{2})+x^{2}y+\tfrac\lambda3y^{3}Vλ​=21​(x2+y2)+x2y+3λ​y3, λ≠0\lambda\ne0λ=0 (Hénon–Heiles is λ=−1\lambda=-1λ=−1), the known integrable members are λ=1\lambda=1λ=1, which separates in the coordinates (y±x)/2(y\pm x)/\sqrt2(y±x)/2​, and λ=6\lambda=6λ=6, which has a second integral that is a polynomial of degree four, quadratic in the momenta.7 For every other λ\lambdaλ there is no second integral that is meromorphic in the complexified coordinates and momenta — in particular no rational or polynomial one (cited: Ito 1985, completed by Morales-Ruiz, Ramis and Simó 2007). Integrals that are only smooth, or defined on part of phase space, are not excluded. So the statement is: within this family, and for integrals of this class, the integrable members are isolated points of the parameter line.

That is all. No measure is put on “the space of all Hamiltonians”: that would need a space, a measure and a theorem, and the course has none of the three. What the two statements show is that integrability is not the default, and that it is not an accident of approximation — Figure 6.9 shows it destroyed by one, and item 2, at λ=−1\lambda=-1λ=−1, proves that meromorphic integrability is lost.

Summary

  • The problem: H=H0(I)+εH1(φ,I)H=H_0(I)+\varepsilon H_1(\varphi,I)H=H0​(I)+εH1​(φ,I) (6.1). Poincaré’s theorem (Theorem 6.3, cited): for H0H_0H0​ non-degenerate and a dense Poincaré set (Definition 6.2) there is no second integral that is a power series in ε\varepsilonε.
  • Small denominators: first-order perturbation theory gives sk=ihk/(k⋅ν)s_k=\ii h_k/(k\cdot\freq)sk​=ihk​/(k⋅ν) (Proposition 6.4), singular on the dense set of resonances; the same resonances force any integral’s leading term to depend on H0H_0H0​ alone (Proposition 6.5, proved at first order).
  • KAM (Theorem 6.8, cited): for analytic, non-degenerate HHH, most tori survive, deformed; they carry Diophantine frequencies (Definition 6.6), which are almost all (Lemma 6.7). For n=2n=2n=2 they trap the orbits between them.
  • Poincaré–Birkhoff (Theorem 6.10, cited): the section map preserves area (Proposition 6.9); a resonant circle breaks into an island chain of elliptic and hyperbolic periodic points, and the hyperbolic points’ crossing curves form homoclinic tangles, the seed of chaos.
  • Hénon–Heiles (6.7): regular →\to→ mixed →\to→ chaotic as E=ε2E=\varepsilon^{2}E=ε2 grows. It is the cubic truncation of the periodic Toda chain (6.8), which stays regular at the same energy.
  • Lyapunov exponents (Definition 6.11) vanish on tori. Genericity, and no more: Poincaré’s theorem, and the isolation of the integrable members of the Hénon–Heiles family.
