Lecture 20

Strings and the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz

What this lecture does

Lecture 19 put one state — the ground state — into the thermodynamic limit. At any temperature above zero there are exponentially many states to choose from, and thermodynamics is the business of counting them. Counting Bethe states is unusually easy, because Lecture 19 already found the slots: a state is a choice of which vacancies are filled. The count gives an entropy, minimising the free energy against it gives one nonlinear integral equation, and the solution of that equation is the exact equation of state of an interacting quantum gas — exact at every temperature and coupling, and measured on an atom chip. The chain costs more, because its roots are not all real: they form strings, and the one equation becomes infinitely many, which collapse again into the Y-system.

20.1 Counting

Everything in this lecture rests on one sentence of Lecture 19: a Bethe state is not a list of rapidities but a choice of quantum numbers from the list of vacancies. Choose a subset, solve (19.1), and the roots come out. So counting states is counting subsets — and that is a problem with an answer. Remark 20.2 (What the count assumes). It assumes, first — the step doing the work — that every way of filling the slots is admissible, with no further extensive restriction within a cell or between cells. A rule against occupying adjacent slots, say, would replace (AB)\binom AB(BA​) by (A−B+1B)\binom{A-B+1}B(BA−B+1​) and change the entropy at leading order. Second, that distinct admissible choices give distinct states and exhaust the spectrum — completeness, which Lecture 15 could only cite. Third, that all choices with the same coarse-grained densities are equally numerous. And fourth, for the chain, the string hypothesis of §20.5, since without it the list of quantum numbers is not known. None of the four is proved here. What is proved is everything downstream of (20.1).

Remark 20.3 (Two familiar limits of one formula). ϑ=0\vartheta=0ϑ=0 and ϑ=1\vartheta=1ϑ=1 both give zero: an empty region and a full one each admit one arrangement. The maximum is ln⁡2\ln2ln2 per slot at ϑ=12\vartheta=\tfrac12ϑ=21​. The exclusion is not in the statistics of the particles but in the Bethe equations, which forbid two equal quantum numbers.

20.8 Why it works, and what it does not prove

There is a second, sharper reason to trust it.
