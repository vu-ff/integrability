Lecture 3

The exterior derivative, Stokes, and the symplectic form

What this lecture does

Course map: G1, step 3 of 9. Calculus on forms, and the one 222-form mechanics is built on. The exterior derivative d\ddd tells a closed form from an exact one. Stokes’ theorem, cited, then shows that the integral of a closed 111-form round a loop depends only on the loop’s class, which is what will make the action variables of Lecture 6 well defined. The second half names the symplectic form, a closed, non-degenerate 222-form, and checks that its sign convention gives Hamilton’s equations.

3.1 The question

Lecture 2 gave p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq a type, a 111-form, and left two requirements on the loop integral (2.8) open: it must not change when the loop is reparametrised, and it must not change when the loop is deformed. So the question of this lecture is

when does the integral of a 111-form round a loop depend only on the loop’s class — and what structure on phase space does p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq come from?

The answer to the first is closedness, with Stokes’ theorem (Corollary 3.13). The answer to the second is the symplectic form ω\sympω, of which p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is a primitive up to sign (§3.6).

3.2 The exterior derivative

We have d\ddd on functions. Extending it to forms of every degree is what turns this algebra into a calculus.

Definition 3.1 (Exterior derivative). On a 000-form (a function), df\dd fdf is Definition 2.8. On a 111-form α=∑iai dxi\alpha=\sum_i a_i\,\dd x^iα=∑i​ai​dxi, dα  =  ∑idai∧dxi  =  ∑i,j∂ai∂xj dxj∧dxi, \dd\alpha \;=\; \sum_i \dd a_i\wedge \dd x^i \;=\;\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial a_i}{\partial x^j}\,\dd x^j\wedge\dd x^i , dα=i∑​dai​∧dxi=i,j∑​∂xj∂ai​​dxj∧dxi,(3.1) and in general d\ddd acts on ∑ai1…ik dxi1∧⋯∧dxik\sum a_{i_1\dots i_k}\,\dd x^{i_1}\wedge\cdots \wedge\dd x^{i_k}∑ai1​…ik​​dxi1​∧⋯∧dxik​ by differentiating each coefficient and wedging d\ddd of it on the left. The formula is written in coordinates, so it is fair to ask whether a different chart would give a different answer. We take the characterisation on trust (cited; da Silva, §6) and use the formula.

In the plane, for α=P dx+Q dy\alpha=P\,\dd x+Q\,\dd yα=Pdx+Qdy, (3.1) gives dα=(∂Q∂x−∂P∂y) dx∧dy, \dd\alpha=\Big(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\Big)\, \dd x\wedge \dd y , dα=(∂x∂Q​−∂y∂P​)dx∧dy,(3.3) using dx∧dx=0\dd x\wedge\dd x=0dx∧dx=0 and dy∧dx=−dx∧dy\dd y\wedge\dd x=-\dd x\wedge\dd ydy∧dx=−dx∧dy. That reading is Stokes’ theorem in miniature, and §3.4 does no more than add such squares up.

Proof. It is enough to check it on a function, since the general case follows by (3.2) and d(dxi)=0\dd(\dd x^i)=0d(dxi)=0. Definition 3.3 (Closed, exact). A kkk-form α\alphaα is closed if dα=0\dd\alpha=0dα=0, and exact if α=dβ\alpha=\dd\betaα=dβ for some (k−1)(k-1)(k−1)-form β\betaβ.

Theorem 3.2 says exact ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ closed. The converse is the interesting direction, and it is local.

Theorem 3.4 (Poincaré lemma cited). A closed form on a ball — or on any region that can be smoothly contracted to a point — is exact. (da Silva, §6; Arnold, §36.)

Everything this course does with forms lives in the gap between Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 3.4: closed and exact differ, and they differ only because of the shape of the region (Figure 3.2). §3.3 is the smallest example, and Lecture 6 is the payoff.

3.3 Closed but not exact: the annulus

This is the smallest interesting fact in the lecture, and the one to remember. It is tempting to read Proposition 3.6 as bookkeeping. It is not:

on the invariant tori of Lecture 5, in R2n\R^{2n}R2n or a cotangent bundle, p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is closed, which is what makes (2.8) depend on the loop only through its class; and it need not be exact there, which is what allows the answer to be nonzero.

The nonzero actions of the oscillator, the pendulum and the Kepler problem exist for the reason Figure 3.3 exhibits — a cycle that does not bound, and a closed form with no primitive along it.

3.4 Pullback, integration, and Stokes’ theorem

Pullback

Forms have one property vectors do not: they can be transported backwards along any smooth map, with no assumption that the map be invertible.

3.5 The symplectic form

Lecture 2 built 222-forms, and §3.2 showed how to tell a closed one. The phase space of mechanics carries one closed 222-form with a special property, and the rest of Part I is built on it. One piece of notation first: a 222-form eats two vectors, and feeding it one leaves a 111-form.

Definition 3.14 (Interior product). For a 222-form β\betaβ and a vector vvv at the same point, ιvβ\iota_v\betaιv​β is the 111-form w↦β(v,w)w\mapsto\beta(v,w)w↦β(v,w). The slot that vvv fills is the first.

Definition 3.15 (Symplectic form, symplectic manifold). A 222-form ω\sympω on a manifold M\phaseM is symplectic if

  1. it is closed: dω=0\dd\symp=0dω=0; and

  2. it is non-degenerate: at each point, if ω(v,w)=0\symp(v,w)=0ω(v,w)=0 for every www, then v=0v=0v=0.

The pair (M,ω)(\phase,\symp)(M,ω) is a symplectic manifold.

Condition 2 says that the linear map v↦ιvωv\mapsto\iota_v\sympv↦ιv​ω from TpMT_p\phaseTp​M to Tp∗MT_p^{*}\phaseTp∗​M has zero kernel. The two spaces have the same dimension, so v  ⟼  ιvωis an isomorphism TpM  →  ∼    Tp∗M. v\;\longmapsto\;\iota_v\symp \quad\text{is an isomorphism } T_p\phase\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;T_p^{*}\phase . v⟼ιv​ωis an isomorphism Tp​M∼​Tp∗​M.(3.10) A symplectic form converts vectors into covectors and back, with no choices made — the job an inner product does in Euclidean space, done by an antisymmetric object.

  • The action variables can be defined. A closed 111-form integrates to the same number over any two loops that deform into one another (Corollary 3.13). 3.7 Caveats, in one place

  • Status. Three results are cited: that d\ddd does not depend on the chart, the Poincaré lemma (Theorem 3.4), and Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 3.11). Deformation invariance (Corollary 3.13) is proved from Stokes, so it is proved modulo a cited theorem, and so is everything later that rests on it. Reparametrisation invariance (Proposition 3.9) is proved in a reading.

  • A deformation carries orientations. Corollary 3.13 needs a deformation, not merely two loops that together bound a region. The circle r=2r=2r=2 traversed anticlockwise and the circle r=1r=1r=1 traversed clockwise bound the annulus between them as point sets, yet the angle form (3.4) integrates to 2π2\pi2π over the first and to −2π-2\pi−2π over the second.

Summary

  • The exterior derivative. d∘d=0\dd\circ\dd=0d∘d=0 (Theorem 3.2), so exact forms are closed. The converse holds on a ball (the Poincaré lemma) and fails on the annulus (Proposition 3.6).
  • Integration. A 111-form integrates over a curve with no further data and independently of the parametrisation, and ∮df=0\oint\dd f=0∮df=0 round any closed curve.
  • Stokes (cited) gives deformation invariance: a closed 111-form integrates to the same number over loops that deform into one another (Corollary 3.13). That is what will make the action variables well defined.
  • The symplectic form is a closed, non-degenerate 222-form. On R2n\R^{2n}R2n it is ∑idqi∧dpi=−d(p⋅dq)\sum_i\dd q_i\wedge\dd p_i=-\dd(p\cdot\dd q)∑i​dqi​∧dpi​=−d(p⋅dq), and its wedge order gives Hamilton’s equations with the right signs (3.13).
