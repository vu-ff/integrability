Lecture 1

Phase space, flows and Poisson brackets

What this lecture does

The mechanics you already know, rewritten so that the next ten lectures can be stated at all. Nothing here is a new physical law: every equation is one you can already write in coordinates. What changes is what the equations are about — a manifold, a vector field on it, and an algebraic operation on functions — and by the end two things follow that coordinates hide. “Conserved quantity” and “symmetry” become the same sentence, as a one-line consequence of the bracket being antisymmetric; and commuting integrals generate commuting flows, which is the engine of the central theorem of Lecture 4.

1.1 Why a manifold

The pendulum’s phase space is a cylinder

A plane pendulum is described by an angle θ\thetaθ and its conjugate momentum ppp. It is tempting to say the phase space is R2\R^{2}R2, and for most purposes nothing goes wrong. But θ\thetaθ and θ+2π\theta+2\piθ+2π are the same configuration, not two different ones, so the honest phase space is M  =  S1×R, \phase \;=\; S^{1}\times\R , M=S1×R,(1.1) a cylinder: the circle of angles, times the line of momenta.

This is not pedantry, and it is worth seeing the difference immediately. Take the Hamiltonian in units where the length, mass and gravity are 111, H(θ,p)=12p2−cos⁡θ, H(\theta,p)=\tfrac12 p^{2}-\cos\theta , H(θ,p)=21​p2−cosθ,(1.2) and ask what the level set H=EH=EH=E looks like for E>1E>1E>1 — a pendulum swinging right over the top.

  • On R2\R^{2}R2: two curves, p=±2(E+cos⁡θ)p=\pm\sqrt{2(E+\cos\theta)}p=±2(E+cosθ)​, each running off to θ=±∞\theta=\pm\inftyθ=±∞ without ever meeting itself. The angle grows without bound, and the trajectory is not periodic — nor is it a bounded set.

  • On the cylinder: each of the two becomes a single closed curve, winding once around. The motion is periodic, with a period you can compute — and Lecture 5 will compute it.

The same disagreement runs the other way below the separatrix: for −1<E<1-1<E<1−1<E<1 the plane shows infinitely many congruent ovals, one around each point (2πk,0)(2\pi k,0)(2πk,0), where the cylinder shows one oval, which is the one physical orbit.

A theorem that says “the motion is periodic” cannot even be stated on the wrong space. Lecture 4, whose whole content is that the motion runs on a torus, is unstateable without this vocabulary, and that is what §§1.1 and 1.2 are for.

Charts

The circle is the smallest space that cannot be covered by one coordinate. The angle θ\thetaθ is a perfectly good coordinate on S1S^1S1 minus a point, but carry it all the way round and it jumps by 2π2\pi2π; and there is no way to choose the cut so that this does not happen somewhere. Two overlapping charts suffice (Figure 1.1), and the price of the second chart is the whole apparatus of a manifold.

Definition 1.1 (Smooth manifold, in the form we need). A smooth manifold M\phaseM of dimension mmm is a space covered by charts — one-to-one maps from open pieces of M\phaseM onto open subsets of Rm\R^{m}Rm — such that wherever two charts overlap, the map between their coordinates is smooth with smooth inverse. A function on M\phaseM is smooth if it is smooth in every chart, which is well defined precisely because the transitions are.

Throughout Part I, M\phaseM is the phase space of a mechanical system, and it is a manifold of dimension m=2nm=2nm=2n, where nnn is the number of degrees of freedom. That is the only thing nnn ever means in this course; where a statement is about a general manifold and 2n2n2n would be misleading, the dimension is called mmm.

We shall not develop the general theory of manifolds — no embeddings, no partitions of unity, no orientability as a topic. Everything this course needs is: a space that looks like Rm\R^{m}Rm in patches, on which it makes sense to differentiate.

Example 1.2 (Three phase spaces, and what they are not).

  • Plane pendulum: configuration space S1S^{1}S1, phase space T∗S1=S1×RT^{*}S^{1}=S^{1}\times\RT∗S1=S1×R, dimension 222.

  • Double pendulum: two angles, so configuration space is the torus T2=S1×S1T^{2}=S^{1}\times S^{1}T2=S1×S1, and phase space is T∗T2=T2×R2T^{*}T^{2}=T^{2}\times\R^{2}T∗T2=T2×R2, dimension 444. Emphatically not R4\R^{4}R4.

  • Free rigid body: a configuration is an orientation, so configuration space is the rotation group SO(3)SO(3)SO(3) and phase space is T∗SO(3)T^{*}SO(3)T∗SO(3), dimension 666. Each of these is a cotangent bundle, “positions and their momenta”, and Lecture 3 will show that every cotangent bundle carries the structure that makes Hamiltonian mechanics work, for free.

1.2 Tangent vectors, vector fields and flows

Two definitions of a tangent vector, and why both are needed

On the cylinder, what is a “velocity”? In Rm\R^{m}Rm the answer is an arrow from one point to another, but on a curved space there is no arrow from a point to a distant point — only an infinitesimal one, at the point. The picture is Figure 1.2: a tangent vector at ppp is the velocity of a curve through ppp.

That picture is correct and it is the one to hold, but it cannot be computed with: two different curves through ppp can have the same velocity, and saying which pairs do requires coordinates and an equivalence relation. The computational definition avoids that by recording not the curve but what the curve does to functions.

Definition 1.3 (Tangent vector). A tangent vector at ppp is a linear map vvv from smooth functions near ppp to R\RR satisfying the Leibniz rule v(fg)=f(p) v(g)+g(p) v(f). v(fg)=f(p)\,v(g)+g(p)\,v(f). v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+g(p)v(f).(1.3) Such a map is called a derivation at ppp. The tangent vectors at ppp form a vector space TpMT_p\phaseTp​M of dimension mmm, and a chart x1,…,xmx^1,\dots,x^mx1,…,xm gives it the basis ∂/∂x1,…,∂/∂xm\partial/\partial x^1,\dots,\partial/\partial x^m∂/∂x1,…,∂/∂xm.

The two pictures are the same one. A curve γ\gammaγ with γ(0)=p\gamma(0)=pγ(0)=p defines a derivation by v(f)  =  ddt∣t=0f(γ(t)), v(f)\;=\;\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big|_{t=0} f(\gamma(t)), v(f)=dtd​​t=0​f(γ(t)),(1.4) and every derivation arises this way. Read (1.4) from right to left and it says: a tangent vector is a directional derivative, which is exactly what a velocity is for. Keep Figure 1.2 as the picture and Definition 1.3 as the tool, and never expect either alone to do both jobs.

Completeness, and why Lecture 4 cares

Theorem 1.5 promises an integral curve only for a short time. It may not last.

Proposition 1.8 (Compactness rescues completeness sketched). A smooth vector field on a compact manifold is complete.

Proof (sketch). Suppose an integral curve exists on a maximal interval [0,T)[0,T)[0,T) with TTT finite. The standard escape argument says such a curve must eventually leave every compact subset — otherwise Theorem 1.5, applied at a limit point, extends it past TTT and contradicts maximality. On a compact M\phaseM there is nothing to leave. Hence T=∞T=\inftyT=∞. 

This looks like a technicality and it is not. In Lecture 4 the proof of the Liouville–Arnold theorem needs the flows of nnn commuting integrals to be defined for all time, so that Rn\R^{n}Rn — and not merely a small neighbourhood of 000 in Rn\R^{n}Rn — acts on a level set. Proposition 1.8 is where that comes from, applied to the level set rather than to M\phaseM, and it is exactly why the compactness hypothesis appears in the theorem. When Lecture 9 finds a system whose level sets are not compact, this step has to be done by hand instead — and can be, there.

The Lie bracket

Two vector fields can be composed as differential operators, but XYXYXY is not a vector field: applied to a product it produces second derivatives and fails (1.3). The antisymmetric combination survives.

Proposition 1.9 (The Lie bracket). For vector fields X,YX,YX,Y, the operator [X,Y]f  =  X(Yf)−Y(Xf) \comm{X}{Y}f\;=\;X(Yf)-Y(Xf) [X,Y]f=X(Yf)−Y(Xf)(1.6) is again a vector field, with components [X,Y]i=X(Yi)−Y(Xi)\comm{X}{Y}^i=X(Y^i)-Y(X^i)[X,Y]i=X(Yi)−Y(Xi) in any chart.

Proof. Linearity is clear. For Leibniz, expand both terms on a product: X(Y(fg))=X(f Yg+g Yf)=f X(Yg)+g X(Yf)+(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf),X\big(Y(fg)\big)=X\big(f\,Yg+g\,Yf\big) = f\,X(Yg)+g\,X(Yf)+(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf),X(Y(fg))=X(fYg+gYf)=fX(Yg)+gX(Yf)+(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf), and the same with XXX and YYY exchanged. The two cross terms (Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf)(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf)(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf) are symmetric under that exchange, so they cancel in the difference, leaving [X,Y](fg)=f [X,Y]g+g [X,Y]f\comm{X}{Y}(fg)=f\,\comm{X}{Y}g+g\,\comm{X}{Y}f[X,Y](fg)=f[X,Y]g+g[X,Y]f. Those cross terms — note that they are products of first derivatives — are the obstruction to the Leibniz rule, and antisymmetry is exactly what removes them. The genuine second-derivative terms disappear separately and for a different reason: in coordinates they are (XiYj−YiXj) ∂i∂jf(X^iY^j-Y^iX^j)\,\partial_i\partial_j f(XiYj−YiXj)∂i​∂j​f, whose coefficient is antisymmetric in i,ji,ji,j while the Hessian is symmetric. The component formula follows by taking f=xif=x^if=xi. 

What the bracket measures is the failure of the two flows to commute: follow XXX for time ttt, then YYY for time sss, then back along each, and the point you return to differs from where you started at order tststs, by [X,Y]\comm{X}{Y}[X,Y]. We need only the extreme case, and we need it as an input to Lecture 4.

Theorem 1.10 (Commuting fields, commuting flows cited). Two complete vector fields have commuting flows, φtX∘φsY=φsY∘φtX\varphi^X_t\circ\varphi^Y_s=\varphi^Y_s\circ\varphi^X_tφtX​∘φsY​=φsY​∘φtX​ for all s,ts,ts,t, if and only if [X,Y]=0\comm{X}{Y}=0[X,Y]=0. (Arnold, Ch. 8.)

1.3 Hamilton’s equations as one vector field

Now specialise to M=T∗Rn\phase=T^{*}\R^{n}M=T∗Rn with coordinates q1,…,qn,p1,…,pnq_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_nq1​,…,qn​,p1​,…,pn​, and let HHH be a Hamiltonian. One consequence is immediate and is worth drawing. Apply (1.8) to HHH itself: the two terms cancel in pairs, so XHH=0\hvf{H}H=0XH​H=0. Energy is conserved, and geometrically:

XH\hvf{H}XH​ is everywhere tangent to the level sets of HHH.

A trajectory can never leave the level set it starts on (Figure 1.5). That much is unconditional. How much more it buys depends on the level, and the distinction is worth stating now, because Lecture 4 builds a hypothesis out of it.

At a point where dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0 — equivalently, by (1.8), where XH≠0\hvf{H}\ne0XH​=0 — the level set is locally a smooth surface of dimension 2n−12n-12n−1. At a critical point of HHH it need not be a surface at all.

  • One degree of freedom, on a level where dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0: each connected component is a curve, and since XH\hvf{H}XH​ is a nowhere-zero field tangent to it, that component is a single orbit. This is the entire solution of the problem — but note both hypotheses. A level set with several components is several orbits, and a level through a critical point is not covered at all.

  • n≥2n\ge2n≥2: a single conserved quantity confines the motion to a (2n−1)(2n-1)(2n−1)-dimensional surface, again only where dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0, and no further. That is why Lecture 4 asks for nnn integrals rather than one — and why it asks for the level set to be regular.

The qualification is not hypothetical: the pendulum’s level E=1E=1E=1 in §1.7 crosses itself at a critical point, and it is the lecture’s principal worked example.

1.4 The Poisson bracket

For smooth functions f,gf,gf,g on T∗RnT^{*}\R^{n}T∗Rn, {f,g}  =  ∑i=1n(∂f∂qi∂g∂pi−∂f∂pi∂g∂qi),so{qi,pj}=δij. \pb{f}{g}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} -\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \pb{q_i}{p_j}=\delta_{ij}. {f,g}=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂f​∂pi​∂g​−∂pi​∂f​∂qi​∂g​),so{qi​,pj​}=δij​.(1.9)

The bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric {f,g}=−{g,f}\pb{f}{g}=-\pb{g}{f}{f,g}=−{g,f}, and satisfies the Jacobi identity and the Leibniz rule {f,{g,h}}+{g,{h,f}}+{h,{f,g}}=0,{fg,h}=f{g,h}+g{f,h}. \pb{f}{\pb{g}{h}}+\pb{g}{\pb{h}{f}}+\pb{h}{\pb{f}{g}}=0, \qquad \pb{fg}{h}=f\pb{g}{h}+g\pb{f}{h}. {f,{g,h}}+{g,{h,f}}+{h,{f,g}}=0,{fg,h}=f{g,h}+g{f,h}.(1.10)

Proof. Bilinearity and antisymmetry are visible in (1.9), and Leibniz is the product rule applied to each derivative. Jacobi takes a line of bookkeeping, and it is worth doing here rather than deferring it, because §1.5 proves the course’s central identity from it.

Write z1,…,z2nz^{1},\dots,z^{2n}z1,…,z2n for all the coordinates at once and {u,v}=∑α,β∂αu  Jαβ  ∂βv,Jαβ={zα,zβ}, \pb{u}{v}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\partial_\alpha u\;J^{\alpha\beta}\;\partial_\beta v, \qquad J^{\alpha\beta}=\pb{z^\alpha}{z^\beta}, {u,v}=α,β∑​∂α​uJαβ∂β​v,Jαβ={zα,zβ},(1.11) so that JJJ is a constant and antisymmetric matrix — that is all of (1.9) the argument uses. Expand the cyclic sum. Every term carries a second derivative of exactly one of f,g,hf,g,hf,g,h, so the terms fall into three classes, and it is enough to kill one: the others follow by relabelling f,g,hf,g,hf,g,h cyclically. Only two properties of JJJ were used, that it is constant and antisymmetric, so the Jacobi identity has nothing to do with the particular pairing of each qqq with its own ppp. Lecture 3 meets the same fact in geometric clothes: antisymmetry becomes ω\sympω being a 222-form, and constant coefficients make dω=0\dd\symp=0dω=0 automatically. The bracket is what turns §1.3’s geometry back into calculation. For any observable fff, the chain rule along a trajectory together with (1.7) gives dfdt=∑i(∂f∂qiq˙i+∂f∂pip˙i)={f,H}. \frac{\dd f}{\dd t} =\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i +\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\right) =\pb{f}{H}. dtdf​=i∑​(∂qi​∂f​q˙​i​+∂pi​∂f​p˙​i​)={f,H}.(1.12)

Definition 1.13 (First integral). fff is a first integral — a conserved quantity — if {f,H}=0\pb{f}{H}=0{f,H}=0.

So HHH generates time evolution, and (1.12) is the frozen convention of this course: df/dt={f,H}\dd f/\dd t=\pb{f}{H}df/dt={f,H}, with the bracket in that order. Every sign in Part I follows from this choice together with (1.9), and §1.5 is where that bill arrives.

Noether, in one line

The condition {f,H}=0\pb{f}{H}=0{f,H}=0 is antisymmetric in its two arguments, so it vanishes for the pair (f,H)(f,H)(f,H) exactly when it vanishes for (H,f)(H,f)(H,f). Reading (1.12) both ways:

fff is conserved by the flow of HHH   ⟺  \iff⟺ HHH is invariant under the flow of fff.

That is Noether’s theorem, at this level of generality, and it is a one-line consequence of antisymmetry. A conserved quantity is a symmetry; they are not two facts related by a theorem but one fact read in two directions. Angular momentum is conserved because HHH is rotation-invariant, and — the same sentence — rotations are a symmetry because angular momentum is conserved.

1.5 From functions to vector fields: the engine

The second proof locates the content: the engine is the Jacobi identity, wearing different clothes. The map f↦Xff\mapsto\hvf{f}f↦Xf​ is an anti-homomorphism of Lie algebras, and it is one because the bracket satisfies Jacobi — which is also, as Lecture 3 will show, the same statement as dω=0\dd\symp=0dω=0.

The minus sign is not a typo

What the course actually uses

Corollary 1.16 (Commuting integrals, commuting flows). If {Fi,Fj}=0\pb{F_i}{F_j}=0{Fi​,Fj​}=0 then [XFi,XFj]=0\comm{\hvf{F_i}}{\hvf{F_j}}=0[XFi​​,XFj​​]=0, and if in addition the fields are complete, their flows commute.

Proof. X{Fi,Fj}=X0=0\hvf{\pb{F_i}{F_j}}=\hvf{0}=0X{Fi​,Fj​}​=X0​=0, so Theorem 1.15 gives [XFi,XFj]=0\comm{\hvf{F_i}}{\hvf{F_j}}=0[XFi​​,XFj​​]=0; then apply Theorem 1.10. 

Corollary 1.16 is the only use Part I ever makes of Theorem 1.15, and it does not see the sign: zero is zero either way. That is worth saying plainly, because it tells you how much to worry about a minus sign that has just been laboured over — for our purposes, not at all. It is laboured over because it must be printed correctly, not because the course’s conclusions depend on it.

What the corollary buys is large. It converts an algebraic hypothesis that can be checked — nnn functions whose brackets vanish — into a geometric conclusion, nnn commuting flows, and hence an action of the abelian group Rn\R^{n}Rn on each level set. That action is the whole machine of the Liouville–Arnold theorem in Lecture 4, and Theorem 1.15 is what starts it.

1.6 Liouville’s theorem on phase-space volume

Theorem 1.17 (Liouville stated; proved on Sheet 1). The flow of XH\hvf{H}XH​ preserves the phase-space volume dq1⋯dqn dp1⋯dpn\dd q_1\cdots\dd q_n\,\dd p_1\cdots\dd p_ndq1​⋯dqn​dp1​⋯dpn​: for any region RRR and any time ttt for which φt\varphi_tφt​ is defined on all of RRR, vol⁡(φt(R))=vol⁡(R)\vol(\varphi_t(R))=\vol(R)vol(φt​(R))=vol(R).

The qualification is not pedantry, and Example 1.7 is the reason. Hamiltonian flows can be incomplete like any others: on T∗RT^{*}\RT∗R the perfectly smooth H=q2pH=q^{2}pH=q2p gives q˙=q2\dot q=q^{2}q˙​=q2, p˙=−2qp\dot p=-2qpp˙​=−2qp, whose solutions q(t)=q0/(1−q0t)q(t)=q_0/(1-q_0t)q(t)=q0​/(1−q0​t) escape in finite time, so φ1\varphi_1φ1​ is not defined anywhere with q0≥1q_0\ge1q0​≥1 and vol⁡(φ1(R))\vol(\varphi_1(R))vol(φ1​(R)) need not be a number at all. What the theorem says is that volume is preserved wherever the flow exists — and on a compact level set, Proposition 1.8 says that is everywhere.

The reason is one line. A flow changes volume at a rate given by the divergence of its vector field, and from (1.8) div⁡XH=∑i(∂∂qi∂H∂pi−∂∂pi∂H∂qi)=0 \operatorname{div}\hvf{H} =\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} -\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right)=0 divXH​=i∑​(∂qi​∂​∂pi​∂H​−∂pi​∂​∂qi​∂H​)=0(1.16) by equality of mixed partials. Hamiltonian flow is incompressible. Note what is not claimed: regions keep their volume, but they need not keep their shape, and generically they are stretched and folded beyond recognition (Figure 1.6) — which is how a volume-preserving flow can look thoroughly chaotic, as Lecture 6 will show that it does.

Lecture 3 will restate Theorem 1.17 in a single line, once the volume is recognised as ωn/n!\symp^{n}/n!ωn/n! for the symplectic form ω\sympω, and Lecture 6 will use it to argue that trajectories cannot simply settle down.

1.7 Worked phase portraits

For one degree of freedom §1.3 has already solved every problem qualitatively: away from the critical points of HHH, the orbits are the connected components of its level curves, traversed in the direction of XH\hvf{H}XH​. Two portraits are worth having by heart — and the second is interesting precisely where that qualification bites.

The harmonic oscillator, H=12(p2+ν2q2)H=\tfrac12(p^{2}+\freq^{2}q^{2})H=21​(p2+ν2q2). For E>0E>0E>0 the level sets are ellipses about the origin, all closed, all traversed clockwise; the period is 2π/ν2\pi/\freq2π/ν and does not depend on which ellipse. That independence is special, and Lecture 5 will see it break. The level E=0E=0E=0 is the origin alone — the one critical point, an equilibrium and not an ellipse.

The pendulum, H=12p2−cos⁡θH=\tfrac12p^{2}-\cos\thetaH=21​p2−cosθ on the cylinder (1.1), is the richer picture (Figure 1.7) and the course’s standing example. Three regimes, separated by one curve:

  • −1<E<1-1<E<1−1<E<1: libration. Closed curves encircling (θ,p)=(0,0)(\theta,p)=(0,0)(θ,p)=(0,0); the bob swings back and forth.

  • E=1E=1E=1: the separatrix. Here p2=2(1+cos⁡θ)=4cos⁡2(θ/2)p^{2}=2(1+\cos\theta)=4\cos^{2}(\theta/2)p2=2(1+cosθ)=4cos2(θ/2), so p=±2cos⁡(θ/2)p=\pm2\cos(\theta/2)p=±2cos(θ/2) for θ∈(−π,π)\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)θ∈(−π,π) — the range the cylinder coordinate runs over, and the range on which cos⁡(θ/2)≥0\cos(\theta/2)\ge0cos(θ/2)≥0 makes the square root come out without a modulus. This level is the one place where the warning of §1.3 bites: θ=π\theta=\piθ=π, p=0p=0p=0 is a critical point of HHH, so the level set is not a curve there — two branches cross — and it is not one orbit. It is three: the equilibrium itself, and the two curves that leave it and return to it, each taking infinitely long at both ends. Nothing on this level is periodic, though it is drawn as a closed curve on the page.

  • E>1E>1E>1: rotation. Curves winding around the cylinder, ppp never changing sign; the bob goes over the top for ever.

The separatrix is where the two families of closed curves fail to be deformable into one another, and it is the first sign of something this course returns to repeatedly: the interesting structure of an integrable system sits at the places where its level sets change character. Lecture 5 computes the period in both regimes and watches it diverge at the separatrix.

Summary

  • Phase space is a manifold. The pendulum’s is the cylinder S1×RS^{1}\times\RS1×R, not R2\R^{2}R2 (1.1), and on the cylinder a rotating pendulum has one periodic orbit rather than infinitely many curves.
  • Dynamics is a vector field. Hamilton’s equations are the single field XH\hvf{H}XH​ of (1.8); since XHH=0\hvf{H}H=0XH​H=0, trajectories are trapped on level sets of HHH.
  • Completeness. Integral curves can run out of time (Example 1.7); on a compact manifold they cannot (Proposition 1.8). Lecture 4 needs exactly this, on a level set.
  • The bracket. df/dt={f,H}\dd f/\dd t=\pb{f}{H}df/dt={f,H} (1.12), and a first integral is {f,H}=0\pb{f}{H}=0{f,H}=0. By antisymmetry alone, “fff is conserved by HHH” and “HHH is invariant under fff” are the same statement — Noether in one line.
  • The engine. X{f,g}=−[Xf,Xg]\hvf{\pb{f}{g}}=-\comm{\hvf{f}}{\hvf{g}}X{f,g}​=−[Xf​,Xg​] (Theorem 1.15), which is the Jacobi identity in disguise. The minus is forced by df/dt={f,H}\dd f/\dd t=\pb{f}{H}df/dt={f,H} and is not a typo; and the only use Part I makes of the identity — commuting integrals give commuting flows, Corollary 1.16 — cannot see it.
  • Liouville. Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space volume (Theorem 1.17), because XH\hvf{H}XH​ is divergence-free. Volume, not shape.
