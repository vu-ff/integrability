Lecture 1

Phase space and flows

What this lecture does

Course map: G1, step 1 of 9. The mechanics you already know, rewritten so that the next eight lectures can be stated at all. Nothing here is a new physical law: every equation is one you can already write in coordinates. What changes is what the equations are about: a space of states that one coordinate patch cannot always cover, and a vector field on it whose integral curves are the motions. Two facts that coordinates hide then follow. A flow on a compact space runs for ever, and two flows commute exactly when a bracket of their vector fields vanishes. Both are inputs to the Liouville–Arnold theorem of Lecture 5.

1.1 Why a manifold

The pendulum’s phase space is a cylinder

A plane pendulum is described by an angle θ\thetaθ and its conjugate momentum ppp. It is tempting to say the phase space is R2\R^{2}R2, and for most purposes nothing goes wrong. But θ\thetaθ and θ+2π\theta+2\piθ+2π are the same configuration, not two different ones, so the honest phase space is M  =  S1×R, \phase \;=\; S^{1}\times\R , M=S1×R,(1.1) a cylinder: the circle of angles, times the line of momenta.

This is not pedantry, and it is worth seeing the difference immediately. Take the Hamiltonian in units where the length, mass and gravity are 111, H(θ,p)=12p2−cos⁡θ, H(\theta,p)=\tfrac12 p^{2}-\cos\theta , H(θ,p)=21​p2−cosθ,(1.2) and ask what the level set H=EH=EH=E looks like for E>1E>1E>1 — a pendulum swinging right over the top.

  • On R2\R^{2}R2: two curves, p=±2(E+cos⁡θ)p=\pm\sqrt{2(E+\cos\theta)}p=±2(E+cosθ)​, each running off to θ=±∞\theta=\pm\inftyθ=±∞ without ever meeting itself. The angle grows without bound, and the trajectory is not periodic — nor is it a bounded set.

  • On the cylinder: each of the two becomes a single closed curve, winding once around. The motion is periodic, with a period you can compute (an exercise; Lecture 6 computes the period of the swinging pendulum).

The same disagreement runs the other way below the separatrix: for −1<E<1-1<E<1−1<E<1 the plane shows infinitely many congruent ovals, one around each point (2πk,0)(2\pi k,0)(2πk,0), where the cylinder shows one oval, which is the one physical orbit.

A theorem that says “the motion is periodic” cannot even be stated on the wrong space. Lecture 5, whose whole content is that the motion runs on a torus, is unstateable without this vocabulary. So the question of this lecture is

where do the states of a mechanical system live, and how do they move — in terms that do not depend on the coordinates?

The answer comes in three steps: the states live on a manifold (this section); motion is a vector field on it, followed along its integral curves (§1.2); and Hamilton’s equations are one such field (§1.3). The pendulum is the worked example throughout, and §1.4 draws its phase portrait.

Charts

The circle is the smallest space that cannot be covered by one coordinate. The angle θ\thetaθ is a perfectly good coordinate on S1S^1S1 minus a point, but carry it all the way round and it jumps by 2π2\pi2π; and there is no way to choose the cut so that this does not happen somewhere. Two overlapping charts suffice (Figure 1.1), and the price of the second chart is the whole apparatus of a manifold.

Definition 1.1 (Smooth manifold, in the form we need). A smooth manifold M\phaseM of dimension mmm is a space covered by charts — one-to-one maps from open pieces of M\phaseM onto open subsets of Rm\R^{m}Rm — such that wherever two charts overlap, the map between their coordinates is smooth with smooth inverse. A function on M\phaseM is smooth if it is smooth in every chart, which is well defined precisely because the transitions are.

Throughout Part I, M\phaseM is the phase space of a mechanical system, and it is a manifold of dimension m=2nm=2nm=2n, where nnn is the number of degrees of freedom. That is the only thing nnn ever means in this course; where a statement is about a general manifold and 2n2n2n would be misleading, the dimension is called mmm.

Reading

Example 1.2 (Three phase spaces, and what they are not).

  • Plane pendulum: configuration space S1S^{1}S1, phase space T∗S1=S1×RT^{*}S^{1}=S^{1}\times\RT∗S1=S1×R, dimension 222.

  • Double pendulum: two angles, so configuration space is the torus T2=S1×S1T^{2}=S^{1}\times S^{1}T2=S1×S1, and phase space is T∗T2=T2×R2T^{*}T^{2}=T^{2}\times\R^{2}T∗T2=T2×R2, dimension 444. Emphatically not R4\R^{4}R4.

  • Free rigid body: a configuration is an orientation, so configuration space is the rotation group SO(3)SO(3)SO(3) and phase space is T∗SO(3)T^{*}SO(3)T∗SO(3), dimension 666. We name it and go no further; SO(3)SO(3)SO(3) is not simply connected, and that is a genuine physical fact — but it is not one this course needs.

Each of these is a cotangent bundle, “positions and their momenta”, and Lecture 4 will show that every cotangent bundle carries the structure that makes Hamiltonian mechanics work, for free.

1.2 Tangent vectors, vector fields and flows

Tangent vectors

On a curved space a velocity cannot be an arrow from one point to a distant one; it is the velocity of a curve through the point (Figure 1.2). What can be computed with is what that velocity does to functions.

Definition 1.3 (Tangent vector). A tangent vector at ppp is a linear map vvv from smooth functions near ppp to R\RR satisfying the Leibniz rule v(fg)=f(p) v(g)+g(p) v(f). v(fg)=f(p)\,v(g)+g(p)\,v(f). v(fg)=f(p)v(g)+g(p)v(f).(1.3) Such a map is called a derivation at ppp. The tangent vectors at ppp form a vector space TpMT_p\phaseTp​M of dimension mmm, and a chart x1,…,xmx^1,\dots,x^mx1,…,xm gives it the basis ∂/∂x1,…,∂/∂xm\partial/\partial x^1,\dots,\partial/\partial x^m∂/∂x1,…,∂/∂xm.

A curve γ\gammaγ with γ(0)=p\gamma(0)=pγ(0)=p gives such a derivation, v(f)  =  ddt∣t=0f(γ(t)), v(f)\;=\;\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\Big|_{t=0} f(\gamma(t)), v(f)=dtd​​t=0​f(γ(t)),(1.4) and every derivation arises this way: a tangent vector is the velocity of a curve, acting on functions as a directional derivative.

Reading

Why both pictures are needed. The picture of Figure 1.2 is correct and it is the one to hold, but it cannot be computed with: two different curves through ppp can have the same velocity, and saying which pairs do requires coordinates and an equivalence relation. The computational definition avoids that by recording not the curve but what the curve does to functions. Keep Figure 1.2 as the picture and Definition 1.3 as the tool, and never expect either alone to do both jobs.

Vector fields, integral curves and flows

Definition 1.4 (Vector field, integral curve, flow). A vector field XXX assigns a tangent vector Xp∈TpMX_p\in T_p\phaseXp​∈Tp​M to each point, smoothly; in a chart, X=∑iXi(x) ∂/∂xiX=\sum_i X^i(x)\,\partial/\partial x^iX=∑i​Xi(x)∂/∂xi. An integral curve of XXX is a curve with γ˙(t)=Xγ(t)\dot\gamma(t)=X_{\gamma(t)}γ˙​(t)=Xγ(t)​. The flow φt\varphi_tφt​ sends a point ppp to γ(t)\gamma(t)γ(t), where γ\gammaγ is the integral curve through ppp at t=0t=0t=0.

In a chart the integral-curve condition is the system x˙i=Xi(x)\dot x^i=X^i(x)x˙i=Xi(x): an ordinary differential equation, which is why the following is assumed rather than proved.

Theorem 1.5 (Existence and uniqueness cited). Through each point of M\phaseM there is exactly one integral curve of a smooth vector field XXX, defined for at least a short time, and it depends smoothly on its starting point. Where both sides are defined, the flow satisfies φt+s=φt∘φs\varphi_{t+s}=\varphi_t\circ\varphi_sφt+s​=φt​∘φs​ and φ0=id⁡\varphi_0=\idφ0​=id.

Reading

Near a point where X≠0X\ne0X=0 the flow can be straightened: there are coordinates in which X=∂/∂x1X=\partial/\partial x^1X=∂/∂x1 and the integral curves are parallel straight lines (Figure 1.4). Nothing local distinguishes one non-vanishing vector field from another — all the content is global, a theme that returns as Darboux’s theorem, in a reading of Lecture 4.

Completeness

Theorem 1.5 promises an integral curve only for a short time. It may not last: on R\RR, x˙=x2\dot x=x^{2}x˙=x2 has, for x0>0x_0>0x0​>0, the solution x(t)=x0/(1−x0t)x(t)=x_0/(1-x_0t)x(t)=x0​/(1−x0​t), which escapes to infinity as t→1/x0t\to1/x_0t→1/x0​, although nothing is wrong with the vector field.

Definition 1.6 (Complete). XXX is complete if every integral curve is defined for all t∈Rt\in\Rt∈R — equivalently, if φt\varphi_tφt​ is defined on all of M\phaseM for every ttt.

Proposition 1.7 (Compactness rescues completeness sketched). A smooth vector field on a compact manifold is complete.

Proof (sketch). Suppose an integral curve exists on a maximal interval [0,T)[0,T)[0,T) with TTT finite. The standard escape argument says such a curve must eventually leave every compact subset — otherwise Theorem 1.5, applied at a limit point, extends it past TTT and contradicts maximality. On a compact M\phaseM there is nothing to leave. Hence T=∞T=\inftyT=∞. 

The Lie bracket

Two vector fields can be composed as differential operators, but XYXYXY is not a vector field: applied to a product it produces second derivatives and fails (1.3). The antisymmetric combination survives.

Proposition 1.8 (The Lie bracket). For vector fields X,YX,YX,Y, the operator [X,Y]f  =  X(Yf)−Y(Xf) \comm{X}{Y}f\;=\;X(Yf)-Y(Xf) [X,Y]f=X(Yf)−Y(Xf)(1.5) is again a vector field, with components [X,Y]i=X(Yi)−Y(Xi)\comm{X}{Y}^i=X(Y^i)-Y(X^i)[X,Y]i=X(Yi)−Y(Xi) in any chart.

Proof. Linearity is clear. For Leibniz, expand both terms on a product: X(Y(fg))=X(f Yg+g Yf)=f X(Yg)+g X(Yf)+(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf),X\big(Y(fg)\big)=X\big(f\,Yg+g\,Yf\big) = f\,X(Yg)+g\,X(Yf)+(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf),X(Y(fg))=X(fYg+gYf)=fX(Yg)+gX(Yf)+(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf), and the same with XXX and YYY exchanged. The two cross terms (Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf)(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf)(Xf)(Yg)+(Xg)(Yf) are symmetric under that exchange, so they cancel in the difference, leaving [X,Y](fg)=f [X,Y]g+g [X,Y]f\comm{X}{Y}(fg)=f\,\comm{X}{Y}g+g\,\comm{X}{Y}f[X,Y](fg)=f[X,Y]g+g[X,Y]f. Those cross terms — note that they are products of first derivatives — are the obstruction to the Leibniz rule, and antisymmetry is exactly what removes them. The genuine second-derivative terms disappear separately and for a different reason: in coordinates they are (XiYj−YiXj) ∂i∂jf(X^iY^j-Y^iX^j)\,\partial_i\partial_j f(XiYj−YiXj)∂i​∂j​f, whose coefficient is antisymmetric in i,ji,ji,j while the Hessian is symmetric. The component formula follows by taking f=xif=x^if=xi. 

What the bracket measures is the failure of the two flows to commute: follow XXX for time ttt, then YYY for time sss, then back along each, and the point you return to differs from where you started at order tststs, by [X,Y]\comm{X}{Y}[X,Y]. We need only the extreme case, and we need it as an input to Lecture 5.

Theorem 1.9 (Commuting fields, commuting flows cited). Two complete vector fields have commuting flows, φtX∘φsY=φsY∘φtX\varphi^X_t\circ\varphi^Y_s=\varphi^Y_s\circ\varphi^X_tφtX​∘φsY​=φsY​∘φtX​ for all s,ts,ts,t, if and only if [X,Y]=0\comm{X}{Y}=0[X,Y]=0. (Arnold, Ch. 8.)

1.3 Hamilton’s equations as one vector field

Now specialise to M=T∗Rn\phase=T^{*}\R^{n}M=T∗Rn with coordinates q1,…,qn,p1,…,pnq_1,\dots,q_n,p_1,\dots,p_nq1​,…,qn​,p1​,…,pn​, and let HHH be a Hamiltonian. Hamilton’s equations q˙i=∂H∂pi,p˙i=−∂H∂qi \dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} q˙​i​=∂pi​∂H​,p˙​i​=−∂qi​∂H​(1.6) are 2n2n2n scalar equations, and they are exactly the statement that the trajectory is an integral curve of the single vector field XH  =  ∑i=1n(∂H∂pi∂∂qi−∂H∂qi∂∂pi). \hvf{H}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_i} -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\right). XH​=i=1∑n​(∂pi​∂H​∂qi​∂​−∂qi​∂H​∂pi​∂​).(1.7) That is the whole of §1.3: a mechanical system is a manifold with a vector field on it, and solving it means following the arrows.

One consequence is immediate and is worth drawing. Apply (1.7) to HHH itself: the two terms cancel in pairs, so XHH=0\hvf{H}H=0XH​H=0. Energy is conserved, and geometrically:

XH\hvf{H}XH​ is everywhere tangent to the level sets of HHH.

A trajectory can never leave the level set it starts on (Figure 1.5). That much is unconditional. How much more it buys depends on the level, and the distinction is worth stating now, because Lecture 5 builds a hypothesis out of it.

At a point where dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0 — equivalently, by (1.7), where XH≠0\hvf{H}\ne0XH​=0 — the level set is locally a smooth surface of dimension 2n−12n-12n−1. At a critical point of HHH it need not be a surface at all.

  • One degree of freedom, on a level where dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0: each connected component is a curve, and since XH\hvf{H}XH​ is a nowhere-zero field tangent to it, that component is a single orbit. This is the entire solution of the problem — but note both hypotheses. A level set with several components is several orbits, and a level through a critical point is not covered at all.

  • n≥2n\ge2n≥2: a single conserved quantity confines the motion to a (2n−1)(2n-1)(2n−1)-dimensional surface, again only where dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0, and no further. That is why Lecture 5 asks for nnn integrals rather than one — and why it asks for the level set to be regular.

The qualification is not hypothetical: the pendulum’s level E=1E=1E=1 in §1.4 crosses itself at a critical point, and it is the lecture’s principal worked example.

1.4 Worked phase portraits

For one degree of freedom §1.3 has already solved every problem qualitatively: away from the critical points of HHH, the orbits are the connected components of its level curves, traversed in the direction of XH\hvf{H}XH​. Two portraits are worth having by heart — and the second is interesting precisely where that qualification bites.

The harmonic oscillator, H=12(p2+ν2q2)H=\tfrac12(p^{2}+\freq^{2}q^{2})H=21​(p2+ν2q2). For E>0E>0E>0 the level sets are ellipses about the origin, all closed, all traversed clockwise; the period is 2π/ν2\pi/\freq2π/ν and does not depend on which ellipse. That independence is special, and Lecture 6 will see it break. The level E=0E=0E=0 is the origin alone — the one critical point, an equilibrium and not an ellipse.

The pendulum, H=12p2−cos⁡θH=\tfrac12p^{2}-\cos\thetaH=21​p2−cosθ on the cylinder (1.1), is the richer picture (Figure 1.6) and the course’s standing example. Three regimes, separated by one curve:

  • −1<E<1-1<E<1−1<E<1: libration. Closed curves encircling (θ,p)=(0,0)(\theta,p)=(0,0)(θ,p)=(0,0); the bob swings back and forth.

  • E=1E=1E=1: the separatrix. Here p2=2(1+cos⁡θ)=4cos⁡2(θ/2)p^{2}=2(1+\cos\theta)=4\cos^{2}(\theta/2)p2=2(1+cosθ)=4cos2(θ/2), so p=±2cos⁡(θ/2)p=\pm2\cos(\theta/2)p=±2cos(θ/2) for θ∈(−π,π)\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)θ∈(−π,π) — the range the cylinder coordinate runs over, and the range on which cos⁡(θ/2)≥0\cos(\theta/2)\ge0cos(θ/2)≥0 makes the square root come out without a modulus. This level is the one place where the warning of §1.3 bites: θ=π\theta=\piθ=π, p=0p=0p=0 is a critical point of HHH, so the level set is not a curve there — two branches cross — and it is not one orbit. It is three: the equilibrium itself, and the two curves that leave it and return to it, each taking infinitely long at both ends. Nothing on this level is periodic, though it is drawn as a closed curve on the page.

  • E>1E>1E>1: rotation. Curves winding around the cylinder, ppp never changing sign; the bob goes over the top for ever.

The separatrix is where the two families of closed curves fail to be deformable into one another, and it is the first sign of something this course returns to repeatedly: the interesting structure of an integrable system sits at the places where its level sets change character. Lecture 6 computes the period of libration, and watches the period diverge as the separatrix is approached from either side.

1.5 What it buys

Three things, each used later.

  • One degree of freedom is solved. Away from the critical points of HHH, the orbits are the connected components of the level curves (§1.3), and §1.4 drew them for the oscillator and the pendulum. Lecture 6 finds, for the closed ones, the variables in which the motion is uniform.

  • With n≥2n\ge2n≥2 degrees of freedom, one conserved quantity is not enough. It confines the motion to a level set of dimension 2n−12n-12n−1 and no further. Lecture 5 asks for nnn of them, and Lecture 2 supplies the language in which “nnn conserved quantities that do not disturb one another” can be written.

  • Two tools for that theorem. Flows on a compact level set run for all time (Proposition 1.7), and flows whose vector fields have vanishing Lie bracket commute (Theorem 1.9). Together they let nnn such flows act on a level set as a single action of Rn\R^{n}Rn, which is where the proof in Lecture 5 starts.

1.6 Caveats, in one place

  • Status. Existence and uniqueness (Theorem 1.5) and commuting flows (Theorem 1.9) are cited, and completeness on a compact manifold (Proposition 1.7) is sketched. The Lie bracket (Proposition 1.8) and XHH=0\hvf{H}H=0XH​H=0 are proved. Some further standard facts are used without proof, among them the tangent-space facts in Definition 1.3 (the dimension, the basis, and that every derivation comes from a curve), the regular-level facts of §1.3 (a regular level set is a smooth surface, by the implicit function theorem; for one degree of freedom a connected piece of a regular level curve is a single orbit), the meaning of the Lie bracket as the order-tststs failure of two flows to commute, the infinite time taken along the separatrix, and the oscillator’s period.

  • Completeness is a hypothesis. Without compactness a smooth vector field need not be complete, as x˙=x2\dot x=x^{2}x˙=x2 shows. Lecture 5 asks for compact level sets for exactly this reason. Where they are not compact, as for the Toda ring of Lecture 9, completeness has to be proved by hand.

  • One field on the cylinder. (1.7) is written in the coordinates (q,p)(q,p)(q,p) of T∗RnT^{*}\R^{n}T∗Rn, and the pendulum uses it in an angle chart (θ,p)(\theta,p)(θ,p). HHH is a function on the cylinder because it is 2π2\pi2π-periodic in θ\thetaθ. On each connected piece of the overlap of two angle charts, the charts differ by a constant shift of θ\thetaθ, with ppp unchanged, and Hamilton’s equations keep their form under such a shift. So (1.7) gives the same vector field in either chart: XH\hvf{H}XH​ is one vector field on the whole cylinder.

Summary

  • Phase space is a manifold. The pendulum’s is the cylinder S1×RS^{1}\times\RS1×R, not R2\R^{2}R2 (1.1), and on the cylinder a rotating pendulum has one periodic orbit rather than infinitely many curves.
  • Dynamics is a vector field. Hamilton’s equations are the single field XH\hvf{H}XH​ of (1.7). Since XHH=0\hvf{H}H=0XH​H=0, trajectories are trapped on level sets of HHH, and for one degree of freedom the regular level curves are the orbits (Figure 1.6).
  • Completeness. Integral curves can run out of time; on a compact manifold they cannot (Proposition 1.7).
  • Commuting flows. Two complete flows commute if and only if the Lie bracket of their fields vanishes (Theorem 1.9).
  • Next: Lecture 2 writes conserved quantities in two languages, the Poisson bracket and differential forms, and turns “the Lie bracket of the flows vanishes” into a statement about the conserved quantities themselves.
