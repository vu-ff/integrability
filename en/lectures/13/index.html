Lecture 13

Three-body consistency: the Yang–Baxter equation

What this lecture does

Course map: P3, the quantum consistency condition. Lecture 11 solved the delta gas with a two-body phase, a number, and Lecture 12 watched the classical Toda chain scatter with numbers at its crossings, which commute. This lecture gives the particles a label. The two-body S-matrix becomes a matrix, the order of the collisions starts to matter, and asking that it not matter gives the Yang–Baxter equation. Yang’s matrix satisfies it, and it is the R-matrix from which Lecture 14 builds the Heisenberg chain’s commuting transfer matrices.

13.1 The question

Numbers commute, so for Lecture 11’s gas and Lecture 12’s chain the two routes of Figure 12.6 could not disagree. Give each particle a spin, and a collision can exchange the spins as well as the momenta. So the question is

when particles carry labels, the S-matrix is a matrix: what makes factorised scattering consistent?

The answer: the Yang–Baxter equation (13.6), which the three-body contact conditions force (Theorem 13.3), and which Yang’s matrix satisfies (Proposition 13.4). The worked example is two labelled particles (Proposition 13.1).

13.4 Factorised scattering

The NNN-body S-matrix is the ordered product of two-body matrices, one per pair of momentum lines, in the order the lines cross, SN=∏crossings, in time orderXab(uab), S_N=\prod_{\text{crossings, in time order}}X_{ab}(u_{ab}), SN​=crossings, in time order∏​Xab​(uab​),(13.7) and by the Yang–Baxter equation every order of crossings gives the same product: the many-body problem is determined by the two-body one. On a ring, carrying one particle round gives, as in Theorem 11.3, a condition — now on a vector of labels: an eigenvalue problem for a product of XXX’s, a transfer matrix, solved by the nested algebraic Bethe ansatz (optional lecture C).

13.5 What it buys

  • The consistency condition of Part II, derived rather than postulated: a factorised three-body wave exists for every incoming amplitude exactly when the two orders of Figure 13.1 agree.

  • Yang’s matrix, R(u)=u−iP\Rmat(u)=u-\ii PR(u)=u−iP up to a scalar, the R-matrix from which Lecture 14 builds the Heisenberg chain’s commuting transfer matrices.

  • Factorised scattering for NNN particles, sketched: with unitarity, the equation makes every order of crossings give the same product (13.7).

13.6 Caveats, in one place

  • Status. Proposition 13.1, Theorem 13.3 and Proposition 13.4 are proved. The NNN-body statement is sketched, with Coxeter’s presentation of SNS_NSN​ cited, and checked for N=4N=4N=4. The Zamolodchikov–Faddeev algebra of the reading is cited.

  • Necessary, not sufficient. The equation is what a consistent factorised ansatz costs. It does not by itself prove that an interaction is non-diffractive.

  • Unitarity is a second condition, X(u)X(−u)=1X(u)X(-u)=\IdX(u)X(−u)=1 for matrices that commute with PPP; Yang’s satisfy it.

  • Without labels the equation is automatic, so Lecture 11’s gas and Lecture 12’s chain never tested it.

13.7 Three lines crossing, and the equation written down

Suppose three-body scattering is a sequence of two-body events. There are two ways for three lines to cross pairwise. Draw each particle’s path in space and time (a world line), with time running up. Three world lines with three different speeds meet pairwise in three crossings, and the middle line can pass either below or above the crossing of the other two (Figure 13.2). The two orders are different sequences of two-body events, and they describe the same three-body collision. They must agree.

This requirement is the Yang–Baxter equation, and it is a condition on the two-body scattering alone. For particles with nothing but a momentum it is automatic: each two-body event then contributes a number (classically, a shift in position; quantum mechanically, a phase), and numbers do not care about order. It bites when the particles carry an internal label — a spin, a colour — that a two-body event can exchange. Then each event is a matrix, matrices do not commute, and the equation is a strong constraint: a system whose two-body scattering fails it cannot scatter factorisably. Yang met it in 1967 as the consistency condition for exactly this kind of many-body scattering; Baxter met it in 1972 in the statistical mechanics of two-dimensional lattices.

The equation does not organise everything. The geometry of Part I — phase space, the Liouville–Arnold theorem, action-angle variables, the failure of integrability — owes it nothing. Nor does all of the algebra: the Lax pairs of Lecture 8 and the reduction behind Calogero–Moser (optional lecture B) do not come from it. What rests on it is one construction: commuting transfer matrices built from an r\rmatr-matrix. There the classical equation is the model-independent condition that makes the bracket consistent — sufficient, not forced, as Lecture 14’s reading explains — and it is realised by the periodic chain of Lecture 9; the quantum equation plays the same part from Lecture 14 on. (The open chain of Lecture 9’s readings has an r\rmatr-matrix bracket too, but of another kind, and its involution does not need the equation.) That construction is the course’s spine from Part I into Part II.

For readers who have met spin: let two particles each carry a spin-12\frac1221​, and let them be fermions with a contact interaction. Their two-body scattering acts on the four-dimensional space C2⊗C2\C^{2}\otimes\C^{2}C2⊗C2 of the pair by XF(u)=−L(u)/(u+i)X_{\mathrm F}(u)=-L(u)/(u+\ii)XF​(u)=−L(u)/(u+i) of (13.3), with numerator L(u)=u 1+i P,L(u)=u\,\Id+\ii\,\perm,L(u)=u1+iP, where P\permP swaps the two spins and uuu is the difference of the two particles’ rapidities, a parametrisation of their momenta. The scalar −1/(u+i)-1/(u+\ii)−1/(u+i) makes XFX_{\mathrm F}XF​ unitary, and it is the same three factors on both sides of the equation below, so the equation can be written with LLL. For three particles, write L12(u)L_{12}(u)L12​(u) for L(u)L(u)L(u) acting on the spins of particles 111 and 222 and as the identity on the third, and so on. The two orders of Figure 13.2 are the two sides of L12(u−v) L13(u) L23(v)=L23(v) L13(u) L12(u−v),L_{12}(u-v)\,L_{13}(u)\,L_{23}(v) =L_{23}(v)\,L_{13}(u)\,L_{12}(u-v),L12​(u−v)L13​(u)L23​(v)=L23​(v)L13​(u)L12​(u−v), an identity between 8×88\times88×8 matrices. 13.9 The Zamolodchikov–Faddeev algebra

Factorised scattering has an algebraic shorthand. Give each asymptotic particle an operator Z(k)Z(k)Z(k) that creates it, with one component per label, and write Za(k)Z_a(k)Za​(k) for Z(k)Z(k)Z(k) with its label in tensor slot aaa (Lecture 9’s leg notation). Postulate the exchange relation Z1(k1) Z2(k2)=X12(u12) Z2(k2) Z1(k1):Z_1(k_1)\,Z_2(k_2)=X_{12}(u_{12})\,Z_2(k_2)\,Z_1(k_1):Z1​(k1​)Z2​(k2​)=X12​(u12​)Z2​(k2​)Z1​(k1​): reordering two neighbours costs one two-body matrix. A product of three can be reversed in two ways, giving X12X13X23 Z3Z2Z1X_{12}X_{13}X_{23}\,Z_3Z_2Z_1X12​X13​X23​Z3​Z2​Z1​ and X23X13X12 Z3Z2Z1X_{23}X_{13}X_{12}\,Z_3Z_2Z_1X23​X13​X12​Z3​Z2​Z1​: the two sides of (13.6). Exchanging twice returns the product to itself only if X(u)X(−u)=1X(u)X(-u)=\IdX(u)X(−u)=1 (for matrices that commute with PPP, as Yang’s do): unitarity. This is the Zamolodchikov–Faddeev algebra (Zamolodchikov and Zamolodchikov 1979; Faddeev 1980) cited. In relativistic field theory, where it was born, the two-body matrix obeys a third condition, crossing symmetry, which relates a particle going forward in time to an antiparticle going backwards. It has no counterpart for the non-relativistic particles of this course, which is one reason field theory is outside it (Lecture 0).

  • Labels (Proposition 13.1): two labelled particles scatter with Yang’s matrix YYY; along the momentum lines X=PYX=PYX=PY, which for bosons is R(u)/(u+i)\Rmat(u)/(u+\ii)R(u)/(u+i) with R(u)=u−iP\Rmat(u)=u-\ii PR(u)=u−iP and u=(k1−k2)/cu=(k_1-k_2)/cu=(k1​−k2​)/c.
  • Three-body consistency (Theorem 13.3): given unitarity, the factorised ansatz has a solution for every incoming amplitude if and only if X12X13X23=X23X13X12X_{12}X_{13}X_{23}=X_{23}X_{13}X_{12}X12​X13​X23​=X23​X13​X12​, the Yang–Baxter equation.
  • Yang’s matrix satisfies it (Proposition 13.4), because the spectral parameters are differences of momenta and add; a harmless-looking variant fails.
  • NNN particles (sketched): unitarity and the equation make the ansatz consistent, and the NNN-body S-matrix is an ordered product of two-body matrices (13.7).
