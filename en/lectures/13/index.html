Lecture 13

What is quantum integrability?

What this lecture does

Classical integrability was a definition with a theorem attached: nnn independent integrals in involution, and under two hypotheses the motion winds round tori. The quantum version has no such definition, and this lecture says why. Counting commuting operators decides nothing, because every Hamiltonian on a finite-dimensional space has as many as there are states. What physicists use instead is a set of criteria: conserved charges that are sums of local pieces, scattering without diffraction, and the statistics of the energy levels. The last can be tested on a laptop, and doing so honestly takes one piece of care that every first attempt leaves out. The lecture is the quantum mirror of Lecture 6.

13.2 The naive definition fails

What “independent” meant classically

Every function of the energy is conserved, {h(H),H}=0\pb{h(H)}{H}=0{h(H),H}=0, so conserved quantities are never scarce. Liouville’s definition (Definition 4.6) asks for more: nnn of them whose differentials are linearly independent (Definition 4.1). That is a condition with content. h(H)h(H)h(H) fails it, since dh(H)=h′(H) dH\dd h(H)=h'(H)\,\dd Hdh(H)=h′(H)dH is parallel to dH\dd HdH; and independence is what makes a level set {F=f}\{F=f\}{F=f} an nnn-dimensional manifold, which the theorem then proves to be a torus. At most nnn functions in involution can be independent (Proposition 3.22), so “nnn” is a sharp number.

One reading makes every system integrable, the other two make none. The count of commuting operators cannot be the definition, and there is no quantum analogue of functional independence to rescue it: the classical notion was a statement about differentials, and a finite matrix has none. This is the remark of §0.3 made precise; Caux and Mossel (2011) call the counting definition “too naive” for this reason cited.

What survives is not how many conserved operators there are but what they look like in the basis in which the system was written down. The projector onto a typical eigenstate of a chain of twenty spins has nonzero entries all over its symmetry block, a block of dimension up to about 10410^{4}104; the Hamiltonian is a sum of twenty terms, each acting on two neighbouring spins. That difference is the next section.

13.3 Local and extensive charges

Densities

From here on the system is a ring of NNN sites, each carrying a spin 12\tfrac1221​: the Hilbert space is (C2)⊗N(\C^{2})^{\otimes N}(C2)⊗N, site N+1N+1N+1 is site 111, and τ\tauτ is the translation that moves every site one step, n↦n+1n\mapsto n+1n↦n+1. Definition 13.2 (Local charge). A density of range rrr is an operator qqq acting on sites 1,…,r1,\dots,r1,…,r. Proposition 13.3 (Local charges are few). For each fixed rrr, the local charges of range at most rrr on a ring of N≥rN\ge rN≥r sites form a vector space of dimension at most 4r4^{r}4r, whatever NNN. Proof. A density of range rrr is an operator on (C2)⊗r(\C^{2})^{\otimes r}(C2)⊗r, a space of dimension 4r4^{r}4r, and q↦Qq\mapsto Qq↦Q is linear. A density of smaller range is one of range rrr that acts trivially on the last sites. 

The bound does not grow with NNN, while the commutant has 2N2^{N}2N dimensions: at any fixed range, the local charges fill a fraction at most 4r/2N4^{r}/2^{N}4r/2N of the conserved operators, which goes to zero as the chain grows. So no family of local charges of one bounded range can be complete; a tower whose ranges grow, like XXX’s below, needs a separate count, and on a ring of NNN sites it has only about NNN members. Locality — not number — is what can tell one Hamiltonian’s conserved quantities from another’s. It is the preferred structure Caux and Mossel ask for.

13.4 Level statistics

Conserved charges are structure one has to find. The spectrum is something one can simply compute, and it carries a signature of integrability.

Spacings

Why should an integrable system be Poissonian and a chaotic one look like a random matrix? Two conjectures say so, supported by much evidence and proved in neither generality: Berry and Tabor (1977) for integrable systems and Bohigas, Giannoni and Schmit (1984) for chaotic ones cited; spin chains were tested from Poilblanc et al. (1993) on. The picture to keep is the trap below in disguise. In an integrable system every level carries quantum numbers, one per conserved charge; levels with different labels do not mix, so their spacings are uncorrelated. In a chaotic system nothing labels the levels, and every pair repels. (The heuristic behind the conjectures is in the reading at the end of this lecture.)

The trap: resolve the symmetries first

Here is the first experiment everyone does, and it gives the wrong answer. Take a chain that nobody believes to be integrable, compute its levels, and histogram the spacings (Figure 13.3, left). The histogram is Poisson.

The reason is block structure. If HHH commutes with a symmetry — here the translation τ\tauτ — it is block diagonal in the symmetry’s eigenbasis, one block per eigenvalue (per momentum kkk). Levels in different blocks sit in different blocks of a matrix: they never mix, so they do not repel, and a list that interleaves several blocks’ spectra is a superposition of independent sequences. Superpose enough independent sequences and the result is Poisson whatever each one was — the same reason independent points are Poisson. (Two GOE spectra superposed already give ⟨r⟩≈0.43\langle r\rangle\approx0.43⟨r⟩≈0.43; eight give 0.390.390.39; check-l13-algebra.py.) So a symmetry left unresolved fakes integrability. The cure is to diagonalise one block at a time and collect statistics inside each.

Which symmetries. Here: translation (momentum k=2πj/Nk=2\pi j/Nk=2πj/N), the total SzS^{z}Sz, the spin flip Sz→−SzS^{z}\to-S^{z}Sz→−Sz, the reflection k→−kk\to-kk→−k, and for isotropic chains SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2). A block is resolved when no symmetry acts within it; Sz=1S^{z}=1Sz=1, 0<k<π0<k<\pi0<k<π and anisotropy achieve it, since the flip and the reflection each move such a block to a different one. That argument is the proof. The generator adds a necessary check, not a sufficient one: no two levels in any block coincide (no spacing below 10−810^{-8}10−8 of the mean). A reader repeating the experiment should do both.

13.5 Working criteria, and their status

The working criteria of quantum integrability, as used in practice and in the rest of this course:

  1. (T)

    Transfer-matrix charges. A family of commuting transfer matrices t(λ)t(\lambda)t(λ), built from a solution of the Yang–Baxter equation, whose logarithmic derivatives give a tower of local conserved charges (Lecture 17).

  2. (S)

    Non-diffractive scattering. When particles are far apart, their momenta are conserved as a set, and many-body scattering factorises into two-body events — the quantum form of E6, and Sutherland’s definition (Lecture 15, Lecture 16).

  3. (P)

    Poisson statistics, in fully resolved symmetry blocks.

They are criteria, not a definition, because no theorem connects them, and there are systems where one holds without another.

  • Charges without (P). The Haldane–Shastry chain, whose exchange falls off as the inverse square of the chord distance, has an explicit commuting family of conserved charges (Talstra and Haldane 1995), though not local ones: they are sums over pairs, triples, … of sites. Its level statistics are neither Poisson nor Wigner–Dyson (Finkel and González-López 2005, quoted by Caux and Mossel) cited. The harmonic oscillator, the most integrable system there is, is not Poisson either: equally spaced levels in one dimension, highly degenerate ones with commensurate frequencies (the reading at the end of this lecture).

  • (P) without (T). A strongly disordered chain in the many-body localised regime shows Poisson statistics and has no transfer matrix; it has quasi-local conserved quantities of its own, but whether that regime survives in infinitely long chains is contested (Abanin, Altman, Bloch and Serbyn 2019; Šuntajs, Bonča, Prosen and Vidmar 2020) cited. And, as Figure 13.3 showed, (P) can be produced by carelessness alone.

  • (S) needs room. Non-diffraction concerns particles that separate — gases on a line, dilute excitations of a chain — and says nothing directly about a finite spectrum (Caux and Mossel) cited.

Symmetry is not integrability, in either direction. The J1J_1J1​–J2J_2J2​ Heisenberg chain has all of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) and translation, so its levels come in large degenerate multiplets; yet within blocks of fixed total spin and momentum its statistics are Wigner–Dyson (⟨r⟩=0.53\langle r\rangle=0.53⟨r⟩=0.53 at J2=0.7J_2=0.7J2​=0.7, N=18N=18N=18, total spin 111; check-l13-algebra.py), and a search at that coupling (N=8,9N=8,9N=8,9) finds no range-333 charge beyond HHH and the total spin (§13.3). Conversely the anisotropic XYZ chain, ∑(JxSxSx+JySySy+JzSzSz)\sum(J_xS^{x}S^{x}+J_yS^{y}S^{y}+J_zS^{z}S^{z})∑(Jx​SxSx+Jy​SySy+Jz​SzSz) with three different couplings, has no continuous symmetry at all and is integrable, by a commuting family of transfer matrices (Baxter, Exactly Solved Models, §10.14) cited. Degeneracies count symmetries; integrability is something else.

What this course does. Every model of Part II that is called integrable is known to have (T) — constructed in full for XXX in Lecture 17, which also verifies quantum Toda’s RLLRLLRLL relation and its classical limit, and cited for the others — or has its charges constructed directly (Lecture 14, Lecture 21). Each lecture names the criterion it uses; none is assumed to imply another.

13.6 The mirror of Lecture 6

Lecture 6 diagnosed classical integrability by a picture: curves or scattered dots in a Poincaré section (Figure 6.9). Figure 13.6 sets it beside this lecture’s. No theorem relates the two rows — one is a property of orbits, the other of a spectrum — but they play the same role, and the two conjectures above claim that they agree where both make sense.

The mirror goes one step further. Classically, a weak perturbation of an integrable system does not destroy it at once: most tori survive (KAM, Theorem 6.8), and chaos grows from the resonances. The quantum question is what Figure 13.5 raises and cannot settle: does a weakly broken integrable chain keep a memory of its charges? In real time it often does, for a while: for suitable initial states, and when the breaking is weak enough to separate the time scales, a system started far from equilibrium first settles to a state set by the approximate charges, and thermalises only much later (Mori, Ikeda, Kaminishi and Ueda 2018) cited. That prethermalisation is the nearest quantum relative of KAM — an analogy, not a theorem — and it is Lecture 23’s.

13.8 Three refinements

Degenerate spectra. If HHH has degenerate eigenvalues — because of a symmetry, say — the operators commuting with it no longer commute with each other, and one looks instead for a maximal commuting family among them. Any such family, simultaneously diagonalised, still consists of the linear combinations of DDD projectors, and the objections of §13.2 return. Von Neumann showed that any commuting family of Hermitian matrices consists of functions of a single one of them (cited by Caux and Mossel) cited.

Which ensemble. The momentum blocks of §13.4 are complex Hermitian, which would suggest the s2s^{2}s2 repulsion of the unitary ensemble. But reflection combined with complex conjugation maps each block to itself, and that antiunitary symmetry, squaring to +1+1+1, makes the real, orthogonal ensemble the right reference — which is what Figure 13.4 shows. In general P(s)P(s)P(s) vanishes like sss, s2s^{2}s2 or s4s^{4}s4 — Dyson’s index 111, 222 or 444 — for the orthogonal (an antiunitary symmetry squaring to +1+1+1), unitary (none) and symplectic (one squaring to −1-1−1) ensembles. Lecture 21 meets all three again, in the ground states of the Calogero–Sutherland model.

Classes. Caux and Mossel propose a classification by how fast the number of nonzero coefficients of the charges grows with NNN when they are expanded in a preferred tensor-product basis of operators, such as the Pauli strings (∑nSnz\sum_nS^{z}_n∑n​Snz​ has NNN of them, although as a matrix it has exponentially many nonzero entries): XXX is “linear”, Haldane–Shastry “quasi-polynomial”, and a generic chain’s conserved operators beyond 111, HHH and its symmetries are expected to need exponentially many cited. It is a proposal; this course uses only its moral, which is Proposition 13.3’s.

13.9 Why Poisson, and why Wigner–Dyson

Berry and Tabor. For an integrable system with nnn degrees of freedom semiclassical quantisation puts the levels at E=K(I)E=K(I)E=K(I) with the actions on a lattice, I=ℏ(m+α)I=\hbar(m+\alpha)I=ℏ(m+α), m∈Znm\in\Z^{n}m∈Zn (E3, with the Maslov shifts α\alphaα of Lecture 15). The levels in a window of energy are the lattice points in a thin shell between two surfaces K=EK=EK=E and K=E+δEK=E+\delta EK=E+δE; for n≥2n\ge2n≥2 and a curved, generic KKK, successive lattice points met in energy order come from unrelated parts of the lattice, and Berry and Tabor (1977) argued that their spacings are then Poisson distributed cited. The argument fails where the lattice is seen coherently. For harmonic oscillators KKK is linear in III: with one degree of freedom the levels are equally spaced (a “picket fence”); with commensurate frequencies they are highly degenerate; with incommensurate ones they are neither, and their spacing law depends on the arithmetic of the frequency ratio — but it is not Poisson either (Berry and Tabor treat both cases). The conjecture is for generic integrable systems.

Bohigas, Giannoni and Schmit. For a system whose classical limit is chaotic, the conjecture (1984) is that the spectral fluctuations are those of the random-matrix ensemble with the same antiunitary symmetry: orthogonal with a time reversal squaring to +1+1+1, unitary without one, symplectic with one squaring to −1-1−1 cited. Semiclassical arguments through periodic orbits support it; there is no proof.

Spin chains have no classical limit with NNN fixed, so neither conjecture applies to them as stated; that they nevertheless follow the same pattern is an empirical fact, tested since the early 1990s and on every chain studied in this course, and it is why (P) is a criterion and not a theorem.
