Lecture 4

Liouville integrability and the Liouville–Arnold theorem

What this lecture does

The central theorem of Part I. On a 2n2n2n-dimensional phase space, nnn independent integrals in involution force the motion onto an nnn-dimensional torus, and on that torus the motion is a straight line traversed at constant speed. We state the theorem with every hypothesis visible, prove it in six stages, say at which stage each hypothesis is spent, and read off what the motion on a torus looks like. A final section, for reading, shows that the theorem is only local: the tori can be glued together in a twisted way.

4.1 Independence and involution

Everything in this lecture is built from three results already proved.

  • Xgf={f,g}\hvf{g}f=\pb{f}{g}Xg​f={f,g}, from Xgh={h,g}\hvf{g}h=\pb{h}{g}Xg​h={h,g} (Definition 1.14); so {f,g}=0\pb{f}{g}=0{f,g}=0 says that fff is constant along the flow of ggg.

Definition 4.1 (Independence). 4.2 Liouville integrable systems: the algebraic definition

Definition 4.6 (Liouville integrable). A Hamiltonian system (M,ω,H)(\phase,\symp,H)(M,ω,H) with dim⁡M=2n\dim\phase=2ndimM=2n is Liouville integrable if there are nnn independent functions F1,…,FnF_1,\dots,F_nF1​,…,Fn​ in involution such that HHH is a function of them, H=h(F1,…,Fn)H=h(F_1,\dots,F_n)H=h(F1​,…,Fn​). By Proposition 3.22, nnn is the most one can ask for. Nothing in the definition mentions compactness, and that is not an oversight: it is an algebraic condition on a list of functions, checked by computing brackets and a rank. Lecture 9 will verify it for the open Toda chain, whose level sets are not compact, and the theorem of the next section will then not apply as stated.

4.3 The theorem, hypothesis by hypothesis

Theorem 4.8 (Liouville–Arnold proved modulo Lemma 4.11 and Theorem 1.10). Let (M,ω)(\phase,\symp)(M,ω) be a symplectic manifold of dimension 2n2n2n, and F1,…,FnF_1,\dots,F_nF1​,…,Fn​ functions in involution. Fix f∈Rnf\in\R^{n}f∈Rn, let Σ\SigmaΣ be a connected component of the level set Mf\phase_fMf​, and suppose

  1. (I)

    dF1,…,dFn\dd F_1,\dots,\dd F_ndF1​,…,dFn​ are linearly independent at every point of Σ\SigmaΣ; and

  2. (C)

    Σ\SigmaΣ is compact.

4.5 Quasi-periodic motion and resonance

The theorem says the motion is a straight line on a torus. It does not say the motion is periodic, and the difference is the subject of Lecture 6.

Definition 4.16 (Resonance). A frequency vector ν∈Rn\freq\in\R^{n}ν∈Rn is resonant if k⋅ν=0k\cdot\freq=0k⋅ν=0 for some nonzero k∈Znk\in\Z^{n}k∈Zn, and non-resonant otherwise. A torus is resonant if its frequency vector is.

A change of lattice basis replaces ν\freqν by BνB\freqBν with BBB an integer matrix of determinant ±1\pm1±1, and k⋅ν=0k\cdot\freq=0k⋅ν=0 becomes (B−Tk)⋅(Bν)=0(B^{-\mathsf T}k)\cdot(B\freq)=0(B−Tk)⋅(Bν)=0: resonance is a property of the torus, not of the basis. For n=2n=2n=2 it says exactly that ν1/ν2\freq_1/\freq_2ν1​/ν2​ is rational (or a frequency vanishes).

Proposition 4.17 (Winding on T2T^{2}T2). Let φ(t)=φ(0)+νt\varphi(t)=\varphi(0)+\freq tφ(t)=φ(0)+νt on T2T^{2}T2 with ν2≠0\freq_2\ne0ν2​=0. If ν1/ν2\freq_1/\freq_2ν1​/ν2​ is rational every orbit is closed; if it is irrational every orbit is dense.

Proof (★). Watch the orbit cross the circle φ2=0\varphi_2=0φ2​=0. It returns every 2π/ν22\pi/\freq_22π/ν2​, having advanced φ1\varphi_1φ1​ by 2πα2\pi\alpha2πα, α=ν1/ν2\alpha=\freq_1/\freq_2α=ν1​/ν2​; so the crossings are φ1(m)=φ1(0)+2πmα\varphi_1^{(m)}=\varphi_1^{(0)}+2\pi m\alphaφ1(m)​=φ1(0)​+2πmα mod 2π2\pi2π. If α=a/b\alpha=a/bα=a/b in lowest terms, the bbb-th crossing is the first, and the orbit closes. If α\alphaα is irrational the crossings are all distinct (else mα∈Zm\alpha\in\Zmα∈Z for some m≠0m\ne0m=0). Of any N+1N+1N+1 of them, two lie within 2π/N2\pi/N2π/N of each other on the circle; if they are crossings mmm and m+dm+dm+d, then every ddd returns rotate the circle by a nonzero angle smaller than 2π/N2\pi/N2π/N, and iterating that rotation lands within 2π/N2\pi/N2π/N of any given point. So the crossings are dense in the circle. Every point of T2T^{2}T2 is reached from the circle by flowing for a time less than 2π/∣ν2∣2\pi/\abs{\freq_2}2π/∣ν2​∣, and flowing for a fixed time is continuous, so the orbit is dense in T2T^{2}T2. 

For general nnn the same is true and harder: if ν\freqν is non-resonant every orbit is dense in TnT^{n}Tn, and time averages of continuous functions along it equal their averages over the torus; if there are exactly rrr independent integer relations k⋅ν=0k\cdot\freq=0k⋅ν=0, the closure of every orbit is a torus of dimension n−rn-rn−r (cited: Arnold, §51 B, Corollary 1, and §51 D). So a torus is not an orbit: on a non-resonant torus a single trajectory visits every neighbourhood and never returns exactly, and only when r=n−1r=n-1r=n−1 (and ν≠0\freq\ne0ν=0) is the torus foliated by closed orbits (Figure 4.8).

In the oscillator every torus has the same ν=(ν1,ν2)\freq=(\freq_1,\freq_2)ν=(ν1​,ν2​), so they are all resonant or all not. That is special. Generically ν\freqν depends on fff, resonant and non-resonant tori are interleaved, and Lecture 6 asks which of them survive a perturbation.

4.6 What the theorem does not give

Theorem 4.8 starts from nnn integrals in involution and says nothing about how to find them. For n=1n=1n=1 the energy suffices (given dH≠0\dd H\ne0dH=0 on a dense open set), which is why every one-degree-of-freedom system is integrable. From n=2n=2n=2 on, a second integral is an extra conserved quantity with no reason to exist, and generically it does not: Lecture 6 makes that precise. Symmetry supplies some integrals, as it did for the central force, but the integrable systems of Part I — the Toda chain, Calogero–Moser — have far more integrals than symmetries, and Lecture 7 gives the method that produces them: Lax pairs.

4.7 The theorem is local: Hamiltonian monodromy

Theorem 4.8 describes one regular torus, and Lecture 5 will extend its angle coordinates to a neighbourhood. Can the tori over all regular values be given coordinates at once? Not always. The obstruction is called Hamiltonian monodromy; it has nothing to do with the monodromy matrix of Lecture 8, which shares only the name.

The spherical pendulum

A unit mass on a sphere of unit radius, in unit gravity, with polar angle θ\thetaθ from the upward vertical, azimuth ϕ\phiϕ, and z=cos⁡θz=\cos\thetaz=cosθ its height: H=12pθ2+pϕ22sin⁡2θ+cos⁡θ,J=pϕ.H=\tfrac12p_\theta^{2}+\frac{p_\phi^{2}}{2\sin^{2}\theta}+\cos\theta, \qquad J=p_\phi .H=21​pθ2​+2sin2θpϕ2​​+cosθ,J=pϕ​. JJJ, the angular momentum about the vertical, commutes with HHH by rotational symmetry, and n=2n=2n=2. The level sets are compact, since ∣p∣2≤2(H+1)\abs{p}^{2}\le2(H+1)∣p∣2≤2(H+1). At fixed J=jJ=jJ=j the height moves in the reduced potential Vj(z)=j2/2(1−z2)+zV_j(z)=j^{2}/2(1-z^{2})+zVj​(z)=j2/2(1−z2)+z, whose minima are the circular conical motions: j2=−(1−z2)2/zj^{2}=-(1-z^{2})^{2}/zj2=−(1−z2)2/z with energy E=(3z2−1)/2zE=(3z^{2}-1)/2zE=(3z2−1)/2z, for −1<z<0-1<z<0−1<z<0. These curves bound the image of the energy–momentum map (H,J)(H,J)(H,J) (Figure 4.9). Over each point of these curves the fibre is a single circle; over their common endpoint (−1,0)(-1,0)(−1,0), the stable equilibrium, it is a point.

The unstable equilibrium at the top, z=1z=1z=1, p=0p=0p=0, is the other critical point. There dH=dJ=0\dd H=\dd J=0dH=dJ=0, it has energy 111, and its image (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) is an isolated critical value in the middle of the image. Every other interior point is a regular value with a compact connected fibre (Cushman–Bates, ch. IV, §3.4), a torus by Theorem 4.8. The fibre over (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) contains the equilibrium and the trajectories that leave it and return to it; it is a torus with one cycle pinched to a point (Figure 4.10). So the set of regular values is a region with a puncture, and a loop round the puncture cannot be shrunk.

The period lattice, carried round the loop

Over each regular value there is a period lattice, and it varies continuously. Choose a basis at one point of the loop Γ\GammaΓ and carry it continuously round Γ\GammaΓ. It comes back as a basis of the same lattice, so the two differ by an integer matrix of determinant ±1\pm1±1. Take the basis [δ],[γ][\delta],[\gamma][δ],[γ], with δ\deltaδ the JJJ-orbit and γ\gammaγ the cycle made by flowing along HHH for the first-return time to the same JJJ-orbit and then back along that JJJ-orbit to the starting point. Then carried once round Γ\GammaΓ, positively oriented, the basis returns as ([δ][γ])  ⟼  (10−11)([δ][γ]), \begin{pmatrix}{[\delta]}\\{[\gamma]}\end{pmatrix} \;\longmapsto\; \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}{[\delta]}\\{[\gamma]}\end{pmatrix}, ([δ][γ]​)⟼(1−1​01​)([δ][γ]​),(4.10) (cited: Duistermaat 1980; the matrix, basis and orientation from Cushman–Bates, ch. IV, §5.2). The JJJ-cycle comes back to itself — it is defined everywhere by the symmetry — and the other cycle comes back with a copy of it subtracted (Figure 4.11). Other sources print (1101)\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}(10​11​) for the same system: the entries depend on the basis and the orientation of the loop. The invariant content is that the matrix is a non-trivial shear, and it cannot be undone by any other choice of basis. One way to see it numerically: the rotation number, the advance of ϕ\phiϕ in one period of zzz divided by 2π2\pi2π, continued once round Γ\GammaΓ, comes back changed by exactly 111.1

What it means, and why it does not contradict the theorem

The theorem is about one torus and, via Lecture 5, a neighbourhood of one torus, where the lattice basis can always be chosen continuously. Monodromy is about the whole family: if angle coordinates existed on all the tori over the punctured region at once, the basis they define would be carried round Γ\GammaΓ unchanged, and (4.10) says it is not. So the spherical pendulum has no global action–angle coordinates (Duistermaat 1980). The same happens round any focus–focus equilibrium: one at which the linearised pair (H,J)(H,J)(H,J) is, in suitable canonical coordinates, the pair (xpx+ypy, xpy−ypx)(xp_x+yp_y,\ xp_y-yp_x)(xpx​+ypy​, xpy​−ypx​) — a hyperbolic scaling and a rotation — provided its critical value is isolated and the nearby fibres, and the singular one, are compact and connected. The top of the sphere is such a point (cited: the geometric monodromy theorem, Cushman–Bates, ch. X, §2). Theorem 4.8 proves the single-torus statement and Lecture 5 the neighbourhood one; global triviality is false.

The quantum sequel. Quantised, HHH and JJJ commute and have a joint spectrum, a set of points in the (E,ℏm)(E,\hbar m)(E,ℏm)-plane that is locally, as ℏ→0\hbar\to0ℏ→0, a deformed square lattice — that is Bohr–Sommerfeld, Lecture 15. Carry a unit cell of it round (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) and it comes back sheared: the quantum numbers label the actions, which pair with the cycles, so the cell’s edges transform contragrediently to (4.10) — in the conventions displayed there by its inverse transpose (1101)\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)(10​11​), again a non-trivial shear. The joint spectrum has a lattice defect at the puncture that no global quantum numbers can remove (cited: Cushman–Duistermaat 1988).

Summary

  • The definition is algebraic: nnn independent functions in involution on a 2n2n2n-dimensional phase space, one of them HHH (Definition 4.6). Each condition is cheap without the other (Examples 4.4 and 4.5), and compactness is not part of it.
  • The theorem (Theorem 4.8): at a regular value, a compact connected component of the level set is a Lagrangian torus TnT^{n}Tn, and the motion on it is φ(t)=φ(0)+νt\varphi(t)=\varphi(0)+\freq tφ(t)=φ(0)+νt.
  • The proof: tangent commuting flows (the engine, Corollary 1.16), complete by compactness, give an action of Rn\R^{n}Rn; independence and connectedness make it transitive with discrete stabiliser Λ\LambdaΛ; the quotient is Tk×Rn−kT^{k}\times\R^{n-k}Tk×Rn−k, and compactness forces k=nk=nk=n. The table in §4.4 says where each hypothesis is spent.
  • The period lattice Λ\LambdaΛ gives angles φ=2πA−1t\varphi=2\pi A^{-1}tφ=2πA−1t, basis cycles γi\gamma_iγi​ and frequencies ν=2πA−1∇h\freq=2\pi A^{-1}\nabla hν=2πA−1∇h (4.9). p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is closed on the torus (Proposition 4.14), which Lecture 5 needs.
  • Resonance: a torus is not an orbit. Rational frequency ratios give closed orbits, irrational ones dense orbits (Proposition 4.17).
  • The theorem is local (reading): the spherical pendulum’s tori twist round the unstable equilibrium, and there are no global action–angle variables (the reading section on Hamiltonian monodromy).
