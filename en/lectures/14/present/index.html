Lecture 14

The quantum inverse scattering method, and its classical limit

Course map: G2 and G5, P2 quantised. Lecture 10 solved the Heisenberg chain by a guess and left the question of why it is solvable. This lecture answers with Lecture 9’s three moves, quantised. The chain’s Lax operator satisfies an RLLRLLRLL relation with Yang’s R\RmatR-matrix of Lecture 13, the monodromy satisfies RTTRTTRTT, and the transfer matrices commute at all values of the spectral parameter. 14.1 The question

Lecture 9 made the Toda ring’s integrals commute with one identity for one site; Lecture 10 solved the Heisenberg chain by a guess. So the question is

why do the Heisenberg chain’s charges commute, and what classical structure is this the quantisation of?

The answer: Lecture 9’s structure, with commutators for brackets and Yang’s R\RmatR for r\rmatr (Theorems 14.1 to 14.3); and the classical limit of the quantum Toda chain proves that the dictionary is right (Corollary 14.6). The worked example is the Heisenberg chain’s Lax operator.

What RTTRTTRTT gives, and what it does not

The proof used (14.6) and the invertibility of R\RmatR, and not the Yang–Baxter equation. The equation does two other jobs. It supplies Lax operators: by (14.7) the rational R\RmatR, read with a site as its second space, is a Lax operator for its own RLLRLLRLL relation, which is why the Heisenberg chain has one. And it is sufficient, not forced, for the consistency of the RTTRTTRTT relations taken as the definition of an algebra (the readings at the end). Finally, integrability does not require RTTRTTRTT: the free-fermion charges of the Ising chain (Lecture 18) are constructed directly.

14.13 Why the zero-curvature equation is not what is quantised

Lecture 8’s and Lecture 9’s readings derived the conservation of t(λ)\transfer(\lambda)t(λ) from the zero-curvature equation (8.23), L˙n=Vn+1Ln−LnVn\dot L_n=V_{n+1}L_n-L_nV_nL˙n​=Vn+1​Ln​−Ln​Vn​, and Lecture 9 proved, separately, the Sklyanin bracket. The zero-curvature equation is the equation of motion under one Hamiltonian, and in quantum mechanics equations of motion are not postulated: they follow from [⋅,H]\comm{\cdot}{H}[⋅,H] once HHH is known. What has to be quantised is the kinematics, the brackets, and from them the commuting family and its Hamiltonians follow. The site-by-site transport survives as the monodromy (14.5), and the bracket as RTTRTTRTT. The Heisenberg equation for T(λ)\mono(\lambda)T(λ) under HHH is then a consequence, and [H,t(λ)]=0\comm{H}{\transfer(\lambda)}=0[H,t(λ)]=0 is Theorem 14.3, not an input.
