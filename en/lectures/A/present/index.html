Optional lecture A

Quantum chaos and level statistics

Off the path: nothing on it uses this lecture. Lecture 7 told an integrable orbit from a chaotic one by a picture. The quantum substitute is not a count of commuting operators, which decides nothing, but a set of criteria: charges from commuting transfer matrices, scattering without diffraction, and the statistics of the energy levels. The last can be tested on a laptop, and doing so honestly takes one piece of care that every first attempt leaves out. None of the criteria is a theorem.

A.1 The question

Lecture 7 diagnosed classical integrability by a picture (Figure 7.5): at the same energy, an orbit of the Toda ring crosses a Poincaré section on a curve, the torus it winds round cut by the section (Figure 7.2), while most orbits of its cubic truncation, Hénon–Heiles, scatter over an area. A quantum system has no orbits to cut. So the question is

what tells an integrable quantum system from a chaotic one, and what does the test prove?

The answer: not the number of conserved operators (Proposition A.1) but their locality; and, as a test, the level statistics inside one fully resolved symmetry block — Poisson for XXZ, Wigner–Dyson with a next-nearest-neighbour coupling J2=0.5J_2=0.5J2​=0.5 at N=18N=18N=18 (Figure A.2). The test is one of three working criteria, and no general theorem connects them (§A.5). The worked example is Lecture 19’s chain (19.1).

A.2 The naive definition fails

By Lecture 10’s rule of translation (10.1), AAA is conserved when [A,H]=0\comm{A}{H}=0[A,H]=0, and two conserved operators are “in involution” when they commute. Every ingredient of Lecture 5’s definition translates except independence.

What “independent” meant classically

Every function of the energy is conserved, {h(H),H}=0\pb{h(H)}{H}=0{h(H),H}=0, so conserved quantities are never scarce. Liouville’s definition (Definition 5.5) asks for more: nnn of them whose differentials are linearly independent (Definition 5.1). That is a condition with content. h(H)h(H)h(H) fails it, since dh(H)=h′(H) dH\dd h(H)=h'(H)\,\dd Hdh(H)=h′(H)dH is parallel to dH\dd HdH; and independence is what makes a level set {F=f}\{F=f\}{F=f} an nnn-dimensional manifold, which, where compact and connected, the theorem proves to be a torus (Theorem 5.7). At most nnn functions in involution can be independent (Proposition 4.11), so “nnn” is a sharp number.

Now try to say what “DDD independent conserved operators” should mean.

  • Linearly independent. By (a), every Hamiltonian with simple spectrum has DDD of them in involution, D−1D-1D−1 after discarding the identity ∑Πa\sum\Pi_a∑Πa​ (with degenerate levels, take the projectors of any eigenbasis). A random matrix qualifies as well as the XXX chain of Lecture 10.

  • Functionally independent, the classical notion, where A=f(B)A=f(B)A=f(B) counts as dependent, as h(H)h(H)h(H) did. By (b), if the spectrum is simple then every conserved operator is a function of HHH, and no such Hamiltonian has a single integral beyond HHH itself, however regular its dynamics. With degenerate levels, as for XXX, the same happens one step up: every commuting family consists of functions of one operator (von Neumann; the reading at the end of this lecture).

  • Algebraically independent: no nonzero polynomial relation among them. By (c), not even one operator on CD\C^{D}CD is algebraically independent; the projectors satisfy Πa2=Πa\Pi_a^{2}=\Pi_aΠa2​=Πa​ and ΠaΠb=0\Pi_a\Pi_b=0Πa​Πb​=0 besides.

One reading makes every system integrable, the other two make none. The count of commuting operators cannot be the definition, and there is no quantum analogue of functional independence to rescue it: the classical notion was a statement about differentials, and a finite matrix has none. Caux and Mossel (2011) call the counting definition “too naive” for this reason cited.

What survives is not how many conserved operators there are but what they look like in the basis in which the system was written down. The projector onto a typical eigenstate of a chain of twenty spins has nonzero entries all over its symmetry block, a block of dimension up to about 10410^{4}104; the Hamiltonian is a sum of twenty terms, each acting on two neighbouring spins. Lecture 19’s local charges (Definition 19.1) make the difference precise: those of range at most rrr span at most 4r4^{r}4r dimensions, whatever NNN (Proposition 19.2), while the commutant has at least 2N2^{N}2N. At any fixed range, then, the local charges fill a fraction at most 4r/2N4^{r}/2^{N}4r/2N of the conserved operators, which goes to zero as the chain grows. So no family of local charges of one bounded range can be complete; a tower whose ranges grow, like XXX’s (Lecture 15’s reading), needs a separate count, and on a ring of NNN sites it has only about NNN members. Locality — not number — is what can tell one Hamiltonian’s conserved quantities from another’s.

A.3 Level statistics

Spacings

Let E1≤E2≤…E_1\le E_2\le\dotsE1​≤E2​≤… be the eigenvalues of HHH in one symmetry sector, a notion made precise below, and sn=En+1−Ens_n=E_{n+1}-E_nsn​=En+1​−En​ the spacings. The density of levels varies across the spectrum, so before spacings from different places can be compared they are unfolded: replace each EnE_nEn​ by the smoothed count of levels below it, so that the mean spacing is 111 everywhere. Uncorrelated levels. Levels that repel. Now let the Hamiltonian be a generic real symmetric matrix, with no structure. For two levels the whole story is visible.

The factor sss is the whole point. A degeneracy needs two conditions, a=ba=ba=b and c=0c=0c=0, and two conditions on a random point of the plane are met with probability zero and nearly met only rarely: level repulsion, P(s)→0P(s)\to0P(s)→0 as s→0s\to0s→0. For large GOE matrices the spacing distribution is not exactly (A.4), but close to it (the first reading at the end of this lecture measures how close). “Wigner–Dyson statistics” means this shape.

Why should an integrable system be Poissonian and a chaotic one look like a random matrix? Two conjectures say so, supported by much evidence and proved in neither generality: Berry and Tabor (1977) for integrable systems and Bohigas, Giannoni and Schmit (1984) for chaotic ones cited; spin chains were tested from Poilblanc et al. (1993) on. The picture to keep: in an integrable system every level carries quantum numbers, one per conserved charge, and levels with different labels do not mix, so their spacings are uncorrelated; in a chaotic system nothing labels the levels, and every pair repels (the reading at the end of this lecture).

A.4 Resolve the symmetries first

The first experiment everyone does gives the wrong answer. Take a chain that nobody believes to be integrable and histogram its spacings (Figure A.1, left): Poisson.

The reason is block structure. If HHH commutes with a symmetry — here the translation τ\tauτ — it is block diagonal in the symmetry’s eigenbasis, one block per eigenvalue (per momentum kkk). Levels in different blocks sit in different blocks of a matrix: they never mix, so they do not repel, and a list that interleaves several blocks’ spectra is a superposition of independent sequences. Superpose enough independent sequences and the result is Poisson whatever each one was — the same reason independent points are Poisson. (Two GOE spectra superposed already give ⟨r⟩≈0.43\langle r\rangle\approx0.43⟨r⟩≈0.43; eight give 0.390.390.39; check-l13-algebra.py.) So a symmetry left unresolved fakes integrability. The cure is to diagonalise one block at a time and collect statistics inside each.

Which symmetries. Here: translation (momentum k=2πj/Nk=2\pi j/Nk=2πj/N), the total SzS^{z}Sz, the spin flip Sz→−SzS^{z}\to-S^{z}Sz→−Sz, the reflection k→−kk\to-kk→−k, and for isotropic chains SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2). A block is resolved when no remaining unitary symmetry acts within it (an antiunitary one may, and fixes the reference ensemble; the first reading); Sz=1S^{z}=1Sz=1, 0<k<π0<k<\pi0<k<π and anisotropy achieve it, since the flip and the reflection each move such a block to a different one. That argument is the proof. The generator adds a necessary check, not a sufficient one: no two levels in any block coincide (no spacing below 10−810^{-8}10−8 of the mean). A reader repeating the experiment should do both.

A.5 Working criteria, and their status

The working criteria of quantum integrability, as used in practice and on the path of this course:

  1. (T)

    Transfer-matrix charges. A family of commuting transfer matrices t(λ)t(\lambda)t(λ), built from a solution of the Yang–Baxter equation (Lecture 14), whose logarithmic derivatives give a tower of local conserved charges (Lecture 15 and its reading).

  2. (S)

    Non-diffractive scattering. When particles are far apart, their momenta are conserved as a set, and many-body scattering factorises into two-body events — the quantum form of Lecture 12’s Theorem 12.2, and Sutherland’s definition (Lecture 10, Lecture 11, Lecture 13).

  3. (P)

    Poisson statistics, in fully resolved symmetry blocks.

They are criteria, not a definition, because no general theorem connects them, and there are systems where one holds without another.

  • Charges without (P). The Haldane–Shastry chain, whose exchange falls off as the inverse square of the chord distance, has an explicit commuting family of conserved charges (Talstra and Haldane 1995), though not local ones: they are sums over pairs, triples, … of sites. Its level statistics are neither Poisson nor Wigner–Dyson (Finkel and González-López 2005, quoted by Caux and Mossel) cited. The harmonic oscillator is not Poisson either (the reading on Berry and Tabor).

  • (P) without (T). A strongly disordered chain in the many-body localised regime shows Poisson statistics and has no transfer matrix; it has quasi-local conserved quantities of its own, but whether that regime survives in infinitely long chains is contested (Abanin, Altman, Bloch and Serbyn 2019; Šuntajs, Bonča, Prosen and Vidmar 2020) cited. And, as Figure A.1 showed, (P) can be produced by carelessness alone.

  • (S) needs room. Non-diffraction concerns particles that separate — gases on a line, dilute excitations of a chain — and says nothing directly about a finite spectrum (Caux and Mossel) cited.

Symmetry is not integrability, in either direction. The J1J_1J1​–J2J_2J2​ Heisenberg chain has all of SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2) and translation, so its levels come in large degenerate multiplets; yet within blocks of fixed total spin and momentum its statistics are Wigner–Dyson (⟨r⟩=0.53\langle r\rangle=0.53⟨r⟩=0.53 at J2=0.7J_2=0.7J2​=0.7, N=18N=18N=18, total spin 111; check-l13-algebra.py), and a search at that coupling (N=8,9N=8,9N=8,9) finds no range-333 charge beyond HHH and the total spin (the tutorial). Conversely the anisotropic XYZ chain, ∑(JxSxSx+JySySy+JzSzSz)\sum(J_xS^{x}S^{x}+J_yS^{y}S^{y}+J_zS^{z}S^{z})∑(Jx​SxSx+Jy​SySy+Jz​SzSz) with three different couplings, has no continuous symmetry at all and is integrable, by a commuting family of transfer matrices (Baxter, Exactly Solved Models, §10.14) cited. Degeneracies count symmetries; integrability is something else.

On the path, Lecture 14 constructs commuting transfer matrices for XXX and for quantum Toda; for XXX, Lecture 15 obtains the Hamiltonian from their logarithmic derivative, and its reading sketches the local tower. The Ising chain’s charges are constructed directly, with no transfer matrix (Lecture 18’s reading, Proposition 19.8). None of the criteria is assumed to imply another.

A.6 The mirror of Lecture 7

Lecture 7 diagnosed classical integrability by a picture: curves or scattered dots in a Poincaré section (Figure 7.5). Figure A.4 sets it beside this lecture’s. No theorem relates the two rows — one is a property of orbits, the other of a spectrum — but they play the same role, and the two conjectures above claim that they agree where both make sense.

The mirror goes one step further. Classically, a weak perturbation of an integrable system does not destroy it at once: most tori survive (KAM, Theorem 7.8), and chaos grows from the resonances. The quantum question is what Figure A.3 raises and cannot settle: does a weakly broken integrable chain keep a memory of its charges? For a while it often does — the prethermal plateau of Remark 20.2 — and whether it does in a controlled sense is the quantum KAM question, which that remark leaves open.

A.10 Three refinements

Degenerate spectra. If HHH has degenerate eigenvalues — because of a symmetry, say — the operators commuting with it no longer commute with each other, and one looks instead for a maximal commuting family among them. Any such family, simultaneously diagonalised, still consists of the linear combinations of DDD projectors, and the objections of §A.2 return. Von Neumann showed that any commuting family of Hermitian matrices consists of functions of a single one of them (cited by Caux and Mossel) cited.

Which ensemble. The momentum blocks of §A.4 are complex Hermitian, which would suggest the s2s^{2}s2 repulsion of the unitary ensemble. But reflection combined with complex conjugation maps each block to itself, and that antiunitary symmetry, squaring to +1+1+1, makes the real, orthogonal ensemble the right reference — which is what Figure A.2 shows. In general P(s)P(s)P(s) vanishes like sss, s2s^{2}s2 or s4s^{4}s4 — Dyson’s index 111, 222 or 444 — for the orthogonal (an antiunitary symmetry squaring to +1+1+1), unitary (none) and symplectic (one squaring to −1-1−1) ensembles. How close the large-matrix law is to the surmise: pooling 3⋅1043\cdot10^{4}3⋅104 bulk spacings of 100100100 random 600×600600\times600600×600 GOE matrices, the fraction below any sss differs from the surmise’s by 0.0070.0070.007 (check-l13-algebra.py, which requires less than 0.0150.0150.015).

Classes. Caux and Mossel propose a classification by how fast the number of nonzero coefficients of the charges grows with NNN when they are expanded in a preferred tensor-product basis of operators, such as the Pauli strings (∑nSnz\sum_nS^{z}_n∑n​Snz​ has NNN of them, although as a matrix it has exponentially many nonzero entries): XXX is “linear”, Haldane–Shastry “quasi-polynomial”, and a generic chain’s conserved operators beyond 111, HHH and its symmetries are expected to need exponentially many cited. A.11 Why Poisson, and why Wigner–Dyson

Berry and Tabor. For an integrable system with nnn degrees of freedom semiclassical quantisation puts the levels at E=K(I)E=K(I)E=K(I) with the actions on a lattice, I=ℏ(m+α)I=\hbar(m+\alpha)I=ℏ(m+α), m∈Znm\in\Z^{n}m∈Zn (Bohr–Sommerfeld with the Maslov shifts α\alphaα of Lecture 10’s reading). The levels in a window of energy are the lattice points in a thin shell between two surfaces K=EK=EK=E and K=E+δEK=E+\delta EK=E+δE; for n≥2n\ge2n≥2 and a curved, generic KKK, successive lattice points met in energy order come from unrelated parts of the lattice, and Berry and Tabor (1977) argued that their spacings are then Poisson distributed cited. The argument fails where the lattice is seen coherently. For harmonic oscillators KKK is linear in III: with one degree of freedom the levels are equally spaced (a “picket fence”); with commensurate frequencies they are highly degenerate; with incommensurate ones they are neither, and their spacing law depends on the arithmetic of the frequency ratio — but it is not Poisson either (Berry and Tabor treat both cases). The conjecture is for generic integrable systems.

Bohigas, Giannoni and Schmit. For a system whose classical limit is chaotic, the conjecture (1984) is that the spectral fluctuations are those of the random-matrix ensemble with the same antiunitary symmetry: orthogonal with a time reversal squaring to +1+1+1, unitary without one, symplectic with one squaring to −1-1−1 cited. Semiclassical arguments through periodic orbits support it; there is no proof.

Spin chains have no classical limit with NNN fixed, so neither conjecture applies to them as stated; that they nevertheless follow the same pattern is an empirical fact, tested since the early 1990s and on every chain studied in this course, and it is why (P) is a criterion and not a theorem.

A.12 The tori globally: Hamiltonian monodromy

Theorem 5.7 describes one regular torus, and Lecture 6 extends its angle coordinates to a neighbourhood. Can the tori over all regular values be given coordinates at once? Not always. The obstruction is called Hamiltonian monodromy; it has nothing to do with the monodromy matrix of Definition 9.1, which shares only the name.

The spherical pendulum

A unit mass on a sphere of unit radius, in unit gravity, with polar angle θ\thetaθ from the upward vertical, azimuth ϕ\phiϕ, and z=cos⁡θz=\cos\thetaz=cosθ its height: H=12pθ2+pϕ22sin⁡2θ+cos⁡θ,J=pϕ.H=\tfrac12p_\theta^{2}+\frac{p_\phi^{2}}{2\sin^{2}\theta}+\cos\theta, \qquad J=p_\phi .H=21​pθ2​+2sin2θpϕ2​​+cosθ,J=pϕ​. At fixed J=jJ=jJ=j the height moves in the reduced potential Vj(z)=j2/2(1−z2)+zV_j(z)=j^{2}/2(1-z^{2})+zVj​(z)=j2/2(1−z2)+z, whose minima are the circular conical motions: j2=−(1−z2)2/zj^{2}=-(1-z^{2})^{2}/zj2=−(1−z2)2/z with energy E=(3z2−1)/2zE=(3z^{2}-1)/2zE=(3z2−1)/2z, for −1<z<0-1<z<0−1<z<0. The unstable equilibrium at the top, z=1z=1z=1, p=0p=0p=0, is the other critical point. Every other interior point is a regular value with a compact connected fibre (Cushman–Bates, ch. IV, §3.4), a torus by Theorem 5.7. The fibre over (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) contains the equilibrium and the trajectories that leave it and return to it; it is a torus with one cycle pinched to a point (Figure A.7). So the set of regular values is a region with a puncture, and a loop round the puncture cannot be shrunk.

Over each regular value there is a period lattice, and it varies continuously. Choose a basis at one point of the loop Γ\GammaΓ and carry it continuously round Γ\GammaΓ. It comes back as a basis of the same lattice, so the two differ by an integer matrix of determinant ±1\pm1±1. What it means, and why it does not contradict the theorem

The theorem is about one torus and, via Lecture 6, a neighbourhood of one torus, where the lattice basis can always be chosen continuously. Monodromy is about the whole family: if angle coordinates existed on all the tori over the punctured region at once, the basis they define would be carried round Γ\GammaΓ unchanged, and (A.8) says it is not. So the spherical pendulum has no global action–angle coordinates (Duistermaat 1980). The same happens round any focus–focus equilibrium: one at which the linearised pair (H,J)(H,J)(H,J) is, in suitable canonical coordinates, the pair (xpx+ypy, xpy−ypx)(xp_x+yp_y,\ xp_y-yp_x)(xpx​+ypy​, xpy​−ypx​) — a hyperbolic scaling and a rotation — provided its critical value is isolated and the nearby fibres, and the singular one, are compact and connected. The top of the sphere is such a point (cited: the geometric monodromy theorem, Cushman–Bates, ch. X, §2). Theorem 5.7 proves the single-torus statement and Lecture 6 the neighbourhood one; global triviality is false.

The quantum sequel

After quantisation HHH and JJJ still commute, and their joint eigenvalues (E,ℏm)(E,\hbar m)(E,ℏm) form a set of points in the plane. By the Einstein–Brillouin–Keller rule of Lecture 10’s reading, near any regular value the points are the image of the lattice ℏ(n+12,m)\hbar(n+\tfrac12,m)ℏ(n+21​,m) (a libration in the polar angle, a rotation in the azimuth) under the smooth map (I1,I2)↦(E,J)(I_1,I_2)\mapsto(E,J)(I1​,I2​)↦(E,J) inverse to the action map, asymptotically as ℏ→0\hbar\to0ℏ→0: locally a deformed square lattice. The joint spectrum has a lattice defect at the puncture, and no choice of two global quantum numbers labels it smoothly (Cushman and Duistermaat 1988) cited. Classical Hamiltonian monodromy — the obstruction to global action–angle variables — is thus visible in a quantum spectrum.

A.13 Why study exceptional systems?

Integrable systems are exceptional (Lecture 7), so why study them? Because there are two roads from nature to a prediction, and each charges a toll (Figure A.9).

On the left, one writes down the model one believes and solves it approximately: perturbation theory about something simple, numerics, linearisation about a minimum. On the right, one replaces the model by an idealisation that can be solved exactly, and pays in the model instead. A truncation you can solve is worth as much as a truth you cannot, and it is worth more when it is the organising centre of the truth. The harmonic oscillator organises vibrational spectroscopy, and the hydrogen atom organises chemistry, in exactly this way: nobody believes a molecule is a set of oscillators, but every real spectrum is read as a perturbation of that one. Integrable models are the next such centres: exactly solvable, genuinely interacting, and the points from which nearby systems can be studied as perturbations. How far that works is a real question. Lecture 7 answers it classically (the KAM theorem, Theorem 7.8: most of the tori survive a small perturbation, under hypotheses stated there), and Lecture 20 says what is known for a nearly integrable quantum gas (Remark 20.2).

Lecture 7 also adds the caveat that keeps this honest. Integrability is a property of the whole system, not of an approximation to it: truncating the integrable Toda potential at cubic order gives Hénon–Heiles, which is not integrable (Figure 7.5). The right-hand road does not mean “simplify until solvable”; it means finding the exceptional systems exactly as they are, and treating the rest of the world as their perturbation. Nor are the exceptional systems common: in a one-parameter family of potentials that contains Hénon–Heiles, the integrable members are isolated values of the parameter, for conserved quantities of a precise kind (Lecture 7’s reading, which states exactly that much and no more).

  • A local charge is fragile: XXX has Q3Q_3Q3​ (Theorem A.2); at N=8,9N=8,9N=8,9 and the couplings tried, the J1J_1J1​–J2J_2J2​ chain has no range-333 charge beyond HHH and the total spin.
  • Level statistics: Poisson e−se^{-s}e−s against Wigner πs2e−πs2/4\frac{\pi s}{2} e^{-\pi s^{2}/4}2πs​e−πs2/4 (Proposition A.3), ⟨r⟩≈0.386\langle r\rangle\approx0.386⟨r⟩≈0.386 against 0.5310.5310.531 — after every symmetry is resolved (Figure A.1).
