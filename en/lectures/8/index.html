Lecture 8

The classical rrr-matrix, the Sklyanin bracket and the CYBE

What this lecture does

The deepest lecture in Part I, and the hinge of the whole course. Lecture 7 built a machine that manufactures conserved quantities and then admitted it had not delivered what Lecture 4 asked for: conservation is not involution. This lecture pays that debt. A single structural assumption about the Poisson brackets of a Lax matrix — that they are a commutator with one fixed numerical matrix r\rmatr — turns out to imply involution of all the spectral invariants at once, and consistency of that assumption produces the classical Yang–Baxter equation. Part II is, in its entirety, the deformation of the equation proved in §8.6.

8.1 The question, and L7’s IOU

Lecture 7 showed that a Lax pair dL/dt=[M,L]\dd L/\dd t=\comm{M}{L}dL/dt=[M,L] makes the spectrum of LLL constant, so that every tr⁡Lk\tr L^{k}trLk is a first integral. It also warned, in a box, that this is not enough. Lecture 4 requires nnn integrals that are independent and in involution, and isospectrality delivers neither:

  • Independence is a property of the particular model, checked by hand. Lecture 9 does it for Toda.

  • Involution — {tr⁡Ln,tr⁡Lm}=0\pb{\tr L^{n}}{\tr L^{m}}=0{trLn,trLm}=0 — does not follow from dL/dt=[M,L]\dd L/\dd t=\comm{M}{L}dL/dt=[M,L] at all. That equation says the tr⁡Lk\tr L^{k}trLk are constant along the flow of HHH; it says nothing whatever about their brackets with each other.

That is the IOU. Paying it needs new information, and the information cannot be about the dynamics, since involution is a statement about the phase-space brackets of LLL with itself, made without reference to any Hamiltonian. So the question is:

what does one have to assume about {Lab,Lcd}\pb{L_{ab}}{L_{cd}}{Lab​,Lcd​} to force all the tr⁡Lk\tr L^{k}trLk into involution?

The answer, found by Sklyanin, is startlingly cheap, and stating it requires one piece of notation that is worth twenty minutes on its own.

8.2 Tensor legs: the notation, computed twice

Reading

The tensor product itself is a prerequisite for this course (notes/part-I-syllabus.md §2), at the level of two-spin systems in quantum mechanics. What follows is not the tensor product; it is a compression scheme for writing large collections of Poisson brackets, and it is the compression, not the algebra, that students find hard.

The problem the notation solves

LLL is a d×dd\times dd×d matrix whose entries are functions on phase space. The object we need to talk about is the collection of all the brackets {Lab,Lcd},a,b,c,d=1,…,d, \pb{L_{ab}}{L_{cd}},\qquad a,b,c,d=1,\dots,d, {Lab​,Lcd​},a,b,c,d=1,…,d,(8.1) which for d=2d=2d=2 is sixteen numbers and for d=3d=3d=3 is eighty-one. Writing them one at a time is hopeless. The trick is to package them as a single matrix, of size d2×d2d^{2}\times d^{2}d2×d2, acting on V⊗VV\otimes VV⊗V with V=CdV=\C^{d}V=Cd.

Definition 8.1 (Tensor legs). Write EabE_{ab}Eab​ for the matrix unit with a 111 in row aaa, column bbb, and recall (A⊗B)(ac),(bd)=AabBcd(A\otimes B)_{(ac),(bd)}=A_{ab}B_{cd}(A⊗B)(ac),(bd)​=Aab​Bcd​, the row labelled by the pair (a,c)(a,c)(a,c) and the column by (b,d)(b,d)(b,d). The subscript is a leg, not a matrix index: L1\leg{L}{1}L1​ is the whole matrix LLL, placed in the first factor. The right-hand equality in (8.3) is the one to memorise — the entry in row (a,c)(a,c)(a,c) and column (b,d)(b,d)(b,d) is the bracket of the (a,b)(a,b)(a,b) entry with the (c,d)(c,d)(c,d) entry.

Remark 8.2 (The confusion to head off now). L1\leg{L}{1}L1​ and L2\leg{L}{2}L2​ commute as matrices: (L⊗1)(1⊗L)=L⊗L=(1⊗L)(L⊗1)(L\otimes\Id)(\Id\otimes L)=L\otimes L=(\Id\otimes L)(L\otimes\Id)(L⊗1)(1⊗L)=L⊗L=(1⊗L)(L⊗1), because they act in different factors. Their entries do not Poisson-commute: that is exactly what (8.3) records, and if it were zero this lecture would have no subject. Two different brackets are in play — the matrix commutator and the Poisson bracket — and Lecture 1 warned that [⋅,⋅]\comm{\cdot}{\cdot}[⋅,⋅] carries both meanings. Here they sit in the same formula. The involution theorem of §8.4 and the composition argument of §8.8 both turn on [L1,L2]=0\comm{\leg{L}{1}}{\leg{L}{2}}=0[L1​,L2​]=0 at their decisive step, and nothing in this lecture uses {L1,L2}=0\pb{\leg{L}{1}}{\leg{L}{2}}=0{L1​,L2​}=0, which is false.

8.3 The Sklyanin bracket

Definition 8.5 (Sklyanin bracket). A Lax matrix L(λ)L(\lambda)L(λ) satisfies a Sklyanin bracket with classical r\rmatr-matrix r\rmatr if {L1(λ),L2(μ)}  =  [r12(λ−μ),L1(λ)+L2(μ)], \pb{\leg{L}{1}(\lambda)}{\leg{L}{2}(\mu)} \;=\;\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}(\lambda-\mu)}{\leg{L}{1}(\lambda)+\leg{L}{2}(\mu)}, {L1​(λ),L2​(μ)}=[r12​(λ−μ),L1​(λ)+L2​(μ)],(8.7) where r12(λ)\leg{\rmat}{12}(\lambda)r12​(λ) is a matrix on V⊗VV\otimes VV⊗V of numbers — it does not depend on the point of phase space.

Read what (8.7) asserts. On the left is an arbitrary d2×d2d^{2}\times d^{2}d2×d2 matrix of Poisson brackets, d4d^{4}d4 functions on phase space with no reason to be related. On the right is a commutator of one fixed numerical matrix with something built linearly from LLL itself. The assumption is enormously restrictive, and its whole force is that the same r\rmatr works at every point of phase space and for every pair λ,μ\lambda,\muλ,μ.

Two qualifications, both of which the course later cashes:

  • Numerical. If r\rmatr is allowed to depend on the dynamical variables the bracket is called dynamical, and Lecture 11 meets the first real example — Calogero–Moser has no r\rmatr of the constant form. Everything proved in §8.4 survives that generalisation; §8.6 does not.

  • Which r\rmatr? This lecture teaches how to read and check an r\rmatr-matrix, never how to solve for one. Finding r\rmatr from scratch is outside Part I, and Sheet 8 accordingly supplies a candidate and asks you to verify it.

Remark 8.6 (r\rmatr is not arbitrary). Antisymmetry of the Poisson bracket already constrains r\rmatr. Reading (8.3) backwards, {Lab,Lcd}=−{Lcd,Lab}\pb{L_{ab}}{L_{cd}}=-\pb{L_{cd}}{L_{ab}}{Lab​,Lcd​}=−{Lcd​,Lab​} is the matrix statement {L1(λ),L2(μ)}=−P {L1(μ),L2(λ)} P\pb{\leg{L}{1}(\lambda)}{\leg{L}{2}(\mu)}=-\perm\,\pb{\leg{L}{1}(\mu)}{\leg{L}{2}(\lambda)}\,\perm{L1​(λ),L2​(μ)}=−P{L1​(μ),L2​(λ)}P. Substituting (8.7) on both sides and writing r21=P r12 P\leg{\rmat}{21}=\perm\,\leg{\rmat}{12}\,\permr21​=Pr12​P, the two agree as soon as r12(λ)=−r21(−λ). \leg{\rmat}{12}(\lambda)=-\leg{\rmat}{21}(-\lambda). r12​(λ)=−r21​(−λ).(8.8) The rational solution of §8.7 satisfies it, since P P P=P\perm\,\perm\,\perm=\permPPP=P makes r21=r12\leg{\rmat}{21}=\leg{\rmat}{12}r21​=r12​ there, and r(λ)=cP/λ\rmat(\lambda)=c\perm/\lambdar(λ)=cP/λ is odd. §8.6 uses (8.8). This is core material, not an aside: the proof of Theorem 8.11 uses (8.8) to trade r21\leg{\rmat}{21}r21​ for −r12-\leg{\rmat}{12}−r12​, and without it that proof does not start.

8.4 The involution theorem

Theorem 8.7 (Involution from the Sklyanin bracket). If L(λ)L(\lambda)L(λ) satisfies (8.7), then for all n,m≥1n,m\ge1n,m≥1 and all λ,μ\lambda,\muλ,μ at which it holds, {tr⁡L(λ)n,tr⁡L(μ)m}=0. \pb{\tr L(\lambda)^{n}}{\tr L(\mu)^{m}}=0 . {trL(λ)n,trL(μ)m}=0.(8.9)

The qualification is needed because the r\rmatr-matrices that occur have a pole at coincident arguments — the rational one of §8.7 is cP/(λ−μ)c\perm/(\lambda-\mu)cP/(λ−μ) — so (8.7) is an assumption about λ≠μ\lambda\ne\muλ=μ and says nothing at λ=μ\lambda=\muλ=μ. Nothing is lost: the left of (8.9) is continuous in λ\lambdaλ and μ\muμ whenever LLL is, and a continuous function vanishing off the diagonal vanishes on it too.

The proof is three steps and each is short. It is the most important argument in Part I.

Proof. Step 1: the chain rule, in tensor-leg form. Both tr⁡L(λ)n\tr L(\lambda)^{n}trL(λ)n and tr⁡L(μ)m\tr L(\mu)^{m}trL(μ)m are functions of the entries of LLL, so by bilinearity and the Leibniz rule of Lecture 1, {F,G}=∑a,b,c,d∂F∂Lab∂G∂Lcd {Lab,Lcd}.\pb{F}{G}=\sum_{a,b,c,d}\frac{\partial F}{\partial L_{ab}} \frac{\partial G}{\partial L_{cd}}\,\pb{L_{ab}}{L_{cd}} .{F,G}=a,b,c,d∑​∂Lab​∂F​∂Lcd​∂G​{Lab​,Lcd​}. For F=tr⁡LnF=\tr L^{n}F=trLn we have ∂F/∂Lab=n (Ln−1)ba\partial F/\partial L_{ab}=n\,(L^{n-1})_{ba}∂F/∂Lab​=n(Ln−1)ba​, and similarly for GGG. Comparing with (8.3), the double sum is exactly a trace over both legs: {tr⁡L(λ)n,tr⁡L(μ)m}=nm  tr⁡12 ⁣(L1(λ)n−1 L2(μ)m−1 {L1(λ),L2(μ)}). \pb{\tr L(\lambda)^{n}}{\tr L(\mu)^{m}} = nm\;\tr_{12}\!\Big(\leg{L}{1}(\lambda)^{n-1}\,\leg{L}{2}(\mu)^{m-1}\, \pb{\leg{L}{1}(\lambda)}{\leg{L}{2}(\mu)}\Big). {trL(λ)n,trL(μ)m}=nmtr12​(L1​(λ)n−1L2​(μ)m−1{L1​(λ),L2​(μ)}).(8.10)

Step 2: substitute the assumption. Put A=L1n−1L2m−1A=\leg{L}{1}^{n-1}\leg{L}{2}^{m-1}A=L1n−1​L2m−1​ and B=L1+L2B=\leg{L}{1}+\leg{L}{2}B=L1​+L2​. Then (8.7) turns (8.10) into {tr⁡L(λ)n,tr⁡L(μ)m}=nm tr⁡(A [r,B]).\pb{\tr L(\lambda)^{n}}{\tr L(\mu)^{m}}=nm\,\tr\big(A\,\comm{\rmat}{B}\big).{trL(λ)n,trL(μ)m}=nmtr(A[r,B]).

Here is the only place any structure is used, and it is Remark 8.2: L1\leg{L}{1}L1​ and L2\leg{L}{2}L2​ commute as matrices, so AAA — a product of their powers — commutes with BBB. Hence, using cyclicity of the trace twice and AB=BAAB=BAAB=BA once, tr⁡(A[r,B])=tr⁡(ArB)−tr⁡(ABr)=tr⁡(ArB)−tr⁡(rAB)=tr⁡(ArB)−tr⁡(rBA)=tr⁡(ArB)−tr⁡(ArB)=0.\tr\big(A\comm{\rmat}{B}\big)=\tr(A\rmat B)-\tr(AB\rmat) =\tr(A\rmat B)-\tr(\rmat AB) =\tr(A\rmat B)-\tr(\rmat BA) =\tr(A\rmat B)-\tr(A\rmat B)=0. tr(A[r,B])=tr(ArB)−tr(ABr)=tr(ArB)−tr(rAB)=tr(ArB)−tr(rBA)=tr(ArB)−tr(ArB)=0. 

Lecture 7’s IOU is paid. Note how little was spent: the argument never opened r\rmatr, never used the Lax equation, and never mentioned a Hamiltonian. It also never used the particular combination L1+L2\leg{L}{1}+\leg{L}{2}L1​+L2​ — only that it commutes with AAA. That observation costs nothing now and is exactly what §8.8 will need.

Corollary 8.8 (What the model gets). Expanding tr⁡L(λ)k\tr L(\lambda)^{k}trL(λ)k in powers of λ\lambdaλ produces a family of functions in involution, and any Hamiltonian among them has all the others as commuting integrals. Whether there are nnn independent ones is a separate question about the model, settled for Toda in Lecture 9.

8.5 The converse: Babelon–Viallet

Theorem 8.7 is one-way: a Sklyanin bracket gives involution. The converse is true but weaker than one would like.

Theorem 8.9 (Babelon–Viallet cited). If the eigenvalues of LLL are in involution on a neighbourhood where LLL is regular and semisimple, then LLL satisfies {L1,L2}=[r12,L1]−[r21,L2], \pb{\leg{L}{1}}{\leg{L}{2}} =\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}}{\leg{L}{1}}-\comm{\leg{\rmat}{21}}{\leg{L}{2}}, {L1​,L2​}=[r12​,L1​]−[r21​,L2​],(8.11) with r\rmatr in general dynamical — a function on phase space.

Read (8.11) carefully: it is not (8.7). Two independent matrices appear, and the single-commutator form is the special case r21=−r12\leg{\rmat}{21}=-\leg{\rmat}{12}r21​=−r12​. The converse does not deliver that special case, and the difference is not a technicality — it is the difference between a true statement and a false one.

Example 8.10 (Why the strengthening fails). On a canonical (q,p)(q,p)(q,p) plane put D=diag⁡(a,b)D=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)D=diag(a,b) with a≠ba\ne ba=b, and L(q,p)=UDU−1,U=eqE12epE21.L(q,p)=U D U^{-1},\qquad U=e^{qE_{12}}e^{pE_{21}} .L(q,p)=UDU−1,U=eqE12​epE21​. Then LLL is regular semisimple near the origin and its eigenvalues are the constants a,ba,ba,b, so they are in involution for the most trivial of reasons. At the origin ∂qL=[E12,D]=(b−a)E12\partial_qL=\comm{E_{12}}{D}=(b-a)E_{12}∂q​L=[E12​,D]=(b−a)E12​ and ∂pL=[E21,D]=(a−b)E21\partial_pL=\comm{E_{21}}{D}=(a-b)E_{21}∂p​L=[E21​,D]=(a−b)E21​, so {L12,L21}∣0=−(a−b)2≠0.\pb{L_{12}}{L_{21}}\Big|_{0}=-(a-b)^{2}\ne0 .{L12​,L21​}​0​=−(a−b)2=0. Now look at where that number would have to come from in (8.7). At the origin L1+L2=D⊗1+1⊗D\leg{L}{1}+\leg{L}{2}=D\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes DL1​+L2​=D⊗1+1⊗D is diagonal with entries 2a, a+b, a+b, 2b2a,\,a+b,\,a+b,\,2b2a,a+b,a+b,2b, and the required entry sits between the two equal ones. For any matrix r\rmatr whatever, dynamical or not, a commutator with a diagonal matrix has that entry multiplied by the difference of the two diagonal entries — here zero. So no r\rmatr can satisfy (8.7) for this LLL, while (8.11), with its two independent terms, has no such obstruction.

So an r\rmatr-matrix always exists behind involution, but it need not be numerical and it need not put the bracket in the single-commutator form of Definition 8.5. The classification of §8.7 applies to neither. Lecture 11 meets a dynamical r\rmatr in Calogero–Moser, so the caveat is not hypothetical.

8.6 The classical Yang–Baxter equation

The Sklyanin bracket is an assumption about a Poisson bracket, so it must be consistent with the Jacobi identity. Working out what that requires produces the equation the rest of the course is about.

Put three legs in play, V⊗V⊗VV\otimes V\otimes VV⊗V⊗V, write Li\leg{L}{i}Li​ for LLL in leg iii at spectral parameter λi\lambda_iλi​, and abbreviate rij=rij(λi−λj)\leg{\rmat}{ij}=\leg{\rmat}{ij}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)rij​=rij​(λi​−λj​). Two facts do the work: [rij,Lk]=0\comm{\leg{\rmat}{ij}}{\leg{L}{k}}=0[rij​,Lk​]=0 when k∉{i,j}k\notin\{i,j\}k∈/{i,j}, because they act on disjoint legs; and r21=−r12\leg{\rmat}{21}=-\leg{\rmat}{12}r21​=−r12​, which Remark 8.6 showed to be sufficient for antisymmetry of the Poisson bracket but which the bracket does not by itself force. It is therefore a hypothesis of Theorem 8.11, not a consequence of Definition 8.5, and is assumed from here on.

Theorem 8.11 (Jacobi and the CYBE). For a Lax matrix satisfying (8.7) with an r\rmatr obeying the skew condition (8.8), the Jacobi identity for L1,L2,L3\leg{L}{1},\leg{L}{2},\leg{L}{3}L1​,L2​,L3​ is equivalent to [  [r12,r13]+[r12,r23]+[r13,r23]  ,  L1+L2+L3  ]=0. \comm{\;\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}}{\leg{\rmat}{13}} +\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}}{\leg{\rmat}{23}} +\comm{\leg{\rmat}{13}}{\leg{\rmat}{23}}\;} {\;\leg{L}{1}+\leg{L}{2}+\leg{L}{3}\;}=0 . [[r12​,r13​]+[r12​,r23​]+[r13​,r23​],L1​+L2​+L3​]=0.(8.12) In particular it holds whenever r\rmatr satisfies the classical Yang–Baxter equation [r12,r13]+[r12,r23]+[r13,r23]=0. \comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}}{\leg{\rmat}{13}} +\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}}{\leg{\rmat}{23}} +\comm{\leg{\rmat}{13}}{\leg{\rmat}{23}}=0 . [r12​,r13​]+[r12​,r23​]+[r13​,r23​]=0.(8.13)

Proof. Remark 8.12 (Machine-checked). The collection above is exactly the kind of bookkeeping in which a sign is lost. Remark 8.13 (Jacobi does not force the CYBE, and saying so matters). Theorem 8.11 gives (8.12), and (8.13) is sufficient for it, not implied by it: the Jacobi identity only requires the triple sum to commute with L1+L2+L3\leg{L}{1}+\leg{L}{2}+\leg{L}{3}L1​+L2​+L3​, and for a particular LLL that is a weaker demand. What makes (8.13) the right thing to impose is that it mentions no LLL at all. One solution r\rmatr then serves every model whose Lax matrix it fits, which is why the classification of r\rmatr-matrices is a subject and the classification of integrable models is not.

One variant deserves its name in passing. Replacing the zero on the right of (8.13) by a specific r\rmatr-independent term gives the modified classical Yang–Baxter equation, whose solutions generate the same structures over a slightly larger class of Lie algebras. Its theory belongs to Lie-bialgebra and Poisson–Lie theory, which this course does not enter; it is named here so that meeting the phrase elsewhere is not a surprise.

8.7 The rational solution

Proposition 8.14 (The rational r\rmatr-matrix). r(λ)=c P/λ\rmat(\lambda)=c\,\perm/\lambdar(λ)=cP/λ satisfies the CYBE (8.13) for every constant ccc.

Proof. Write λij=λi−λj\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_i-\lambda_jλij​=λi​−λj​. The cancellation is a statement about the three spectral parameters and nothing else, which is why ccc is free: the normalisation of r\rmatr is fixed by the Lax matrix it is paired with, not by the CYBE. Lecture 10 fixes it for the periodic Toda chain; the open chain of Lecture 9 needs a different, constant r\rmatr.

Reading

Two further families solve (8.13): the trigonometric r\rmatr-matrices, in which 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ is replaced by cot⁡λ\cot\lambdacotλ or coth⁡λ\coth\lambdacothλ and the permutation is deformed, and the elliptic ones, built from Weierstrass functions. Belavin and Drinfeld classified the solutions under hypotheses this course has not set up: for r\rmatr valued in g⊗g\glie\otimes\glieg⊗g with g\glieg a finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra, in difference form, and non-degenerate in their technical sense (the residue at the simple pole is proportional to the Casimir of an invariant form), every solution is equivalent to a rational, trigonometric or elliptic one cited, and the three degenerate into one another in limits. Those hypotheses are doing real work — dropped, the statement is false, and already fails for d=1d=1d=1, where every commutator in (8.13) vanishes and any function of λ\lambdaλ with a simple pole at the origin solves it. The same trichotomy will reappear in Lecture 11 as the four Calogero–Moser potentials, and in Part II as XXX, XXZ and XYZ — it is the same list every time, which is not a coincidence.

8.8 Monodromy and transfer matrices

Everything so far concerned a single Lax matrix. A lattice model has one per site, and the object that organises them is a product.

Definition 8.15 (Classical monodromy and transfer matrices). For a chain of NNN sites with Lax matrices L(n)(λ)L^{(n)}(\lambda)L(n)(λ), the monodromy matrix and transfer matrix are T(λ)=L(N)(λ)⋯L(1)(λ),t(λ)=tr⁡T(λ). \mono(\lambda)=L^{(N)}(\lambda)\cdots L^{(1)}(\lambda), \qquad \transfer(\lambda)=\tr\mono(\lambda). T(λ)=L(N)(λ)⋯L(1)(λ),t(λ)=trT(λ).(8.14) The chain is ultralocal if variables at different sites Poisson-commute, {Lab(n),Lcd(m)}=0\pb{L^{(n)}_{ab}}{L^{(m)}_{cd}}=0{Lab(n)​,Lcd(m)​}=0 for n≠mn\ne mn=m.

Remark 8.16 (A naming collision, and it is only that). The monodromy matrix T(λ)\mono(\lambda)T(λ) has nothing to do with the Hamiltonian monodromy of Lecture 4 — the global twisting of a torus bundle. Both words come from “going around”: there, a loop in the space of regular values; here, a circuit of the lattice. There the relationship ends, and a student who met the word in Lecture 4 should expect no connection, because there is none.

Now a point that is easy to slide past. The bracket (8.7) is linear in LLL on the right-hand side, and that form does not survive multiplication: computing {T1,T2}\pb{\leg{\mono}{1}}{\leg{\mono}{2}}{T1​,T2​} for a product of two Lax matrices does not return [r,T1+T2]\comm{\rmat}{\leg{\mono}{1}+\leg{\mono}{2}}[r,T1​+T2​]. The form that does survive is the quadratic one.

Theorem 8.17 (The quadratic bracket composes). Suppose the chain is ultralocal and each site satisfies {L1(n)(λ),L2(n)(μ)}=[r12(λ−μ),L1(n)(λ) L2(n)(μ)]. \pb{\leg{L}{1}^{(n)}(\lambda)}{\leg{L}{2}^{(n)}(\mu)} =\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}(\lambda-\mu)} {\leg{L}{1}^{(n)}(\lambda)\,\leg{L}{2}^{(n)}(\mu)} . {L1(n)​(λ),L2(n)​(μ)}=[r12​(λ−μ),L1(n)​(λ)L2(n)​(μ)].(8.15) Then T\monoT satisfies the same relation, {T1,T2}=[r12,T1T2]\pb{\leg{\mono}{1}}{\leg{\mono}{2}} =\comm{\leg{\rmat}{12}}{\leg{\mono}{1}\leg{\mono}{2}}{T1​,T2​}=[r12​,T1​T2​].

Corollary 8.18 (Commuting transfer matrices). {t(λ),t(μ)}=0\pb{\transfer(\lambda)}{\transfer(\mu)}=0{t(λ),t(μ)}=0 for all λ,μ\lambda,\muλ,μ.

Proof. {t(λ),t(μ)}=tr⁡12{T1,T2}=tr⁡12[r,T1T2]=0\pb{\transfer(\lambda)}{\transfer(\mu)} =\tr_{12}\pb{\leg{\mono}{1}}{\leg{\mono}{2}} =\tr_{12}\comm{\rmat}{\leg{\mono}{1}\leg{\mono}{2}}=0{t(λ),t(μ)}=tr12​{T1​,T2​}=tr12​[r,T1​T2​]=0: the trace of a commutator vanishes. So t(λ)\transfer(\lambda)t(λ) is a generating function for commuting charges: expand it in λ\lambdaλ and every coefficient Poisson-commutes with every other. Lecture 10 builds exactly this for the periodic Toda chain, and Lecture 17 builds its quantum counterpart, where the same expansion produces the Hamiltonian of the Heisenberg spin chain.

Summary

  • Tensor legs. {L1,L2}\pb{\leg{L}{1}}{\leg{L}{2}}{L1​,L2​} is the d2×d2d^{2}\times d^{2}d2×d2 matrix whose (ac),(bd)(ac),(bd)(ac),(bd) entry is {Lab,Lcd}\pb{L_{ab}}{L_{cd}}{Lab​,Lcd​} (Definition 8.1). L1\leg{L}{1}L1​ and L2\leg{L}{2}L2​ commute as matrices while their entries do not Poisson-commute — Remark 8.2, and every proof here turns on it.
  • The Sklyanin bracket. {L1,L2}=[r12,L1+L2]\pb{\leg{L}{1}}{\leg{L}{2}} =\comm{\rmat_{12}}{\leg{L}{1}+\leg{L}{2}}{L1​,L2​}=[r12​,L1​+L2​] with r\rmatr numerical (Definition 8.5).
  • Involution. It implies {tr⁡L(λ)n,tr⁡L(μ)m}=0\pb{\tr L(\lambda)^{n}}{\tr L(\mu)^{m}}=0{trL(λ)n,trL(μ)m}=0 (Theorem 8.7), in three lines, using nothing about r\rmatr. This is Lecture 7’s IOU, paid.
  • The CYBE. Jacobi forces (8.12); the CYBE (8.13) is the LLL-independent sufficient condition one imposes (Remark 8.13), and r=cP/λ\rmat=c\perm/\lambdar=cP/λ solves it (Proposition 8.14).

  • The bridge. R=1+iℏr+O(ℏ2)\Rmat=\Id+\ii\hbar\rmat+O(\hbar^{2})R=1+iℏr+O(ℏ2), and that sign fixes the ordering in the quantum relation (8.17).
