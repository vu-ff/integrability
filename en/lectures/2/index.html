Lecture 2

Differential forms

What this lecture does

A calculus lecture, and the only one in this course. We need one number, ∮γp⋅dq\oint_{\gamma} p\cdot\dd q∮γ​p⋅dq, to depend on the loop γ\gammaγ only through which way it winds — not on how fast it is traversed, not on the coordinates it is written in, and not on the particular loop within its class. Building the machinery that makes that true takes one lecture, and it pays for the rest of Part I: the symplectic form of Lecture 3, the period lattice of Lecture 4, and the action variables of Lecture 5 are all written in the language assembled here.

2.1 Why forms: 111-forms and the cotangent space

The object we are trying to build

In Lecture 5 we shall meet the integral I  =  12π∮γp⋅dq  =  12π∮γ ∑i=1npi dqi, I \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma} p\cdot\dd q \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\,\dd q_i , I=2π1​∮γ​p⋅dq=2π1​∮γ​ i=1∑n​pi​dqi​,(2.1) taken around a closed loop γ\gammaγ on an invariant torus, and everything in Lectures 4 and 5 depends on it being a well-defined number. Three separate things have to be true, and not one of them is obvious:

  1. The integrand must be an object that a curve can integrate, with no extra structure supplied. In particular no length, no angle, no inner product: phase space has no metric, and any construction that secretly uses one is not available to us.

  2. The answer must not change when the curve is reparametrised. A loop traversed quickly and the same loop traversed slowly must give the same number.

  3. The answer must not change when γ\gammaγ is deformed. Otherwise (2.1) labels a curve rather than a torus, and there is no “action variable” at all.

The first two are settled in §§2.1 and 2.5 by getting the type of the integrand right. The third is a theorem, and it is the real content of this lecture: it holds because p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is closed, and the loops it is integrated over are not boundaries. Both words are defined below.

Covectors

Throughout this lecture M\phaseM is a smooth manifold of dimension mmm, and x1,…,xmx^1,\dots,x^mx1,…,xm is a chart on it. In the application M\phaseM is phase space and m=2nm=2nm=2n, with nnn the number of degrees of freedom — nnn is reserved for that throughout the course, so it is not available here.

Recall from §1.2 that a tangent vector at p∈Mp\in\phasep∈M is the velocity of a curve through ppp, equivalently a derivation on functions near ppp, and that these form an mmm-dimensional vector space TpMT_p\phaseTp​M. Remark 2.3 (Why not a vector field?). The components ∂f/∂xi\partial f/\partial x^i∂f/∂xi are also the components of the gradient, so it is fair to ask what is gained. The answer is that the gradient is not defined by them. To turn the collection ∂f/∂xi\partial f/\partial x^i∂f/∂xi into a vector one must raise an index, and that needs an inner product; under a change of coordinates the honest transformation law of ∂f/∂xi\partial f/\partial x^i∂f/∂xi is the one covectors have, not the one vectors have. Phase space carries no inner product, so on M\phaseM the gradient of a function does not exist while its differential does. This is not fastidiousness: it is why p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq makes sense on M\phaseM at all.

2.2 222-forms, oriented area and the wedge product

What we want to measure

A 111-form measures a curve’s worth of information: one vector in, one number out. To integrate over a surface we need a machine taking two vectors, and it should return the area of the parallelogram they span — because that is what a surface integral adds up.

Start in the plane, where the answer is already known from linear algebra: the parallelogram spanned by v=(v1,v2)v=(v^1,v^2)v=(v1,v2) and w=(w1,w2)w=(w^1,w^2)w=(w1,w2) has signed area A(v,w)  =  det⁡(v1w1v2w2)  =  v1w2−v2w1. A(v,w)\;=\;\det\begin{pmatrix} v^1 & w^1\\ v^2 & w^2\end{pmatrix} \;=\; v^1w^2-v^2w^1 . A(v,w)=det(v1v2​w1w2​)=v1w2−v2w1.(2.4) Three properties of AAA are worth naming before we generalise, because they are forced by what “area” means and not chosen for convenience.

  • Bilinearity. Doubling one edge doubles the area; gluing two parallelograms along an edge adds their areas.

  • A(v,v)=0A(v,v)=0A(v,v)=0. A degenerate parallelogram has no area.

  • A(w,v)=−A(v,w)A(w,v)=-A(v,w)A(w,v)=−A(v,w). The area is signed: it records not only how much area there is but which way round the two edges run.

The third property is the one students find arbitrary, so note that it is not an extra assumption: it follows from the first two. Expanding 0=A(v+w,v+w)=A(v,v)+A(v,w)+A(w,v)+A(w,w)0=A(v+w,v+w)=A(v,v)+A(v,w)+A(w,v)+A(w,w)0=A(v+w,v+w)=A(v,v)+A(v,w)+A(w,v)+A(w,w) gives A(v,w)=−A(w,v)A(v,w)=-A(w,v)A(v,w)=−A(w,v) at once. Antisymmetry is what you get for free once you ask that a degenerate parallelogram have zero area.

The wedge product

Two 111-forms produce a 222-form in exactly one natural way.

Remark 2.10 (Degrees run out). On an mmm-dimensional manifold every kkk-form with k>mk>mk>m vanishes: more than mmm vectors in an mmm-dimensional space are linearly dependent, and an alternating multilinear map vanishes whenever its arguments are — for a wedge of 111-forms this is (2.8) with a dependent set of columns. So in the plane 222-forms are top degree, and any 333-form is zero — a fact used without comment every time a computation in R2\R^{2}R2 stops after one step.

2.3 The exterior derivative

We have d\ddd on functions. Extending it to forms of every degree is what turns this algebra into a calculus.

This is the smallest interesting fact in the lecture, and the one to remember. It is tempting to read Proposition 2.17 as bookkeeping. It is not:

p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is closed on an invariant torus, which is what makes (2.1) depend on the loop only through its class; and it need not be exact there, which is what allows the answer to be nonzero.

The two halves do different jobs and neither can be dropped. Were p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq exact on the torus, (2.1) would give I=0I=0I=0 for every cycle on it, by Proposition 2.22. The nonzero actions of the oscillator, the pendulum and the Kepler problem exist for the reason Figure 2.9 exhibits — a cycle that does not bound, and a closed form with no primitive along it.

Reading

Remark 2.18 (Not a universal claim). “Not exact” is what makes a nonzero action possible, not something that holds on every invariant torus. On T∗S1T^{*}S^{1}T∗S1 with H=pH=pH=p, the level p=0p=0p=0 is a perfectly good compact invariant circle on which p dqp\,\dd qpdq restricts to 000 — exact, with action zero, and no contradiction anywhere. What Lecture 5 needs from this lecture is the closedness, which is what makes the period well defined at all.

2.5 Pullback, integration, and Stokes’ theorem

Pullback

Forms have one property vectors do not: they can be transported backwards along any smooth map, with no assumption that the map be invertible.

Definition 2.19 (Pullback). Let F ⁣:N→MF\colon N\to \phaseF:N→M be smooth and let α\alphaα be a 111-form on M\phaseM. Its pullback F∗αF^{*}\alphaF∗α is the 111-form on NNN given by (F∗α)p(v)  =  αF(p)(dFp v), (F^{*}\alpha)_p(v)\;=\;\alpha_{F(p)}\big(\dd F_p\,v\big), (F∗α)p​(v)=αF(p)​(dFp​v),(2.14) where dFp ⁣:TpN→TF(p)M\dd F_p\colon T_pN\to T_{F(p)}\phasedFp​:Tp​N→TF(p)​M is the derivative of FFF. For a 222-form, (F∗β)p(v,w)=βF(p)(dFpv,dFpw)(F^{*}\beta)_p(v,w)=\beta_{F(p)}(\dd F_pv,\dd F_pw)(F∗β)p​(v,w)=βF(p)​(dFp​v,dFp​w), and similarly in any degree.

In practice pullback is substitution: if FFF is given by xi=xi(u)x^i=x^i(u)xi=xi(u), then F∗(dxi)=∑a(∂xi/∂ua) duaF^{*}(\dd x^i)=\sum_a(\partial x^i/\partial u^a)\,\dd u^aF∗(dxi)=∑a​(∂xi/∂ua)dua, and one simply substitutes. Two consequences are worth recording, both because they are used later and because they are the reason the formalism is worth its cost: F∗(df)=d(f∘F),F∗(α∧β)=F∗α∧F∗β,F∗(dα)=d(F∗α). F^{*}(\dd f)=\dd(f\circ F), \qquad F^{*}(\alpha\wedge\beta)=F^{*}\alpha\wedge F^{*}\beta, \qquad F^{*}(\dd\alpha)=\dd (F^{*}\alpha). F∗(df)=d(f∘F),F∗(α∧β)=F∗α∧F∗β,F∗(dα)=d(F∗α).(2.15) The last of these — d\ddd commutes with pullback — is what makes “closed” and “exact” coordinate-independent notions, and Lecture 3 will use the middle one to recognise canonical transformations as the maps satisfying F∗ω=ωF^{*}\symp=\sympF∗ω=ω.

Integration over a curve

Now the type-checking of §2.1 pays off.

Definition 2.20 (Line integral of a 111-form). For a smooth curve γ ⁣:[a,b]→M\gamma\colon[a,b]\to\phaseγ:[a,b]→M and a 111-form α\alphaα, ∫γα  =  ∫abγ∗α  =  ∫abαγ(t)(γ˙(t)) dt. \int_{\gamma}\alpha\;=\;\int_{a}^{b}\gamma^{*}\alpha \;=\;\int_{a}^{b}\alpha_{\gamma(t)}\big(\dot\gamma(t)\big)\,\dd t . ∫γ​α=∫ab​γ∗α=∫ab​αγ(t)​(γ˙​(t))dt.(2.16)

Proposition 2.21 (Reparametrisation invariance). If φ ⁣:[c,d]→[a,b]\varphi\colon[c,d]\to[a,b]φ:[c,d]→[a,b] is smooth, increasing and onto, then ∫γ∘φα=∫γα\int_{\gamma\circ\varphi}\alpha=\int_{\gamma}\alpha∫γ∘φ​α=∫γ​α.

Proof (★). By the chain rule dds(γ∘φ)=γ˙(φ(s)) φ′(s)\tfrac{\dd}{\dd s}(\gamma\circ\varphi)=\dot\gamma(\varphi(s))\, \varphi'(s)dsd​(γ∘φ)=γ˙​(φ(s))φ′(s), so the integrand acquires exactly the factor φ′(s)\varphi'(s)φ′(s) that the substitution rule removes. 

So the answer depends on the curve and its direction, not on the schedule. That was requirement 2 of §2.1, and Definition 2.20 met it by construction: a 111-form is precisely the kind of object whose integral over a curve needs no further data.

Proposition 2.22 (Fundamental theorem). ∫γdf=f(γ(b))−f(γ(a))\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\dd f=f(\gamma(b))-f(\gamma(a))∫γ​df=f(γ(b))−f(γ(a)). In particular ∮γdf=0\oint_{\gamma}\dd f=0∮γ​df=0 for every closed curve.

Proof. df(γ˙)=ddt(f∘γ)\dd f(\dot\gamma)=\tfrac{\dd}{\dd t}(f\circ\gamma)df(γ˙​)=dtd​(f∘γ) by (2.2), and the one-variable fundamental theorem of calculus does the rest. 

Proposition 2.22 is what Proposition 2.17 used, and it is also the statement that an exact form carries no information about loops. Everything interesting therefore lies with forms that are closed and not exact.

Stokes’ theorem

Theorem 2.23 (Stokes cited). Let SSS be a compact oriented surface with boundary ∂S\partial S∂S, carrying the induced orientation, and let α\alphaα be a 111-form defined on a neighbourhood of SSS. Then ∫Sdα  =  ∮∂Sα. \int_{S}\dd\alpha \;=\; \oint_{\partial S}\alpha . ∫S​dα=∮∂S​α.(2.17) The same identity holds in every degree, for a kkk-form on a (k+1)(k+1)(k+1)-dimensional region. (Proof: da Silva, §6; Arnold, §36.)

We do not prove it. What we do instead is say exactly why it is true, because the mechanism is short and is the picture the rest of Part I uses.

The payoff: integrals that depend only on the class of the loop

Corollary 2.26 (Deformation invariance). Let α\alphaα be a closed 111-form on a region UUU, and let F ⁣:S1×[0,1]→UF\colon S^{1}\times[0,1]\to UF:S1×[0,1]→U be a smooth deformation — a family of closed curves γs=F(⋅,s)\gamma_s=F(\cdot,s)γs​=F(⋅,s) inside UUU, running from γ0\gamma_0γ0​ to γ1\gamma_1γ1​. Then ∮γ1α=∮γ0α.\oint_{\gamma_1}\alpha=\oint_{\gamma_0}\alpha .∮γ1​​α=∮γ0​​α.

Proof. Pull α\alphaα back to the cylinder C=S1×[0,1]C=S^{1}\times[0,1]C=S1×[0,1], where it is a 111-form on a 222-dimensional region. It is still closed, because d\ddd commutes with pullback (2.15): d(F∗α)=F∗(dα)=0\dd(F^{*}\alpha)=F^{*}(\dd\alpha)=0d(F∗α)=F∗(dα)=0. The boundary of CCC is its two ends, the top with the orientation induced from CCC and the bottom with the opposite one, so ∂C=S1×{1}−S1×{0}\partial C=S^{1}\times\{1\}- S^{1}\times\{0\}∂C=S1×{1}−S1×{0}. By Theorem 2.23 applied on CCC, ∮γ1α−∮γ0α  =  ∮∂CF∗α  =  ∫Cd(F∗α)  =  0.\oint_{\gamma_1}\alpha-\oint_{\gamma_0}\alpha \;=\;\oint_{\partial C}F^{*}\alpha \;=\;\int_{C}\dd (F^{*}\alpha)\;=\;0 . ∮γ1​​α−∮γ0​​α=∮∂C​F∗α=∫C​d(F∗α)=0. 

Remark 2.27 (Why the statement is about a deformation, not a shape). It is tempting to say instead “if γ0\gamma_0γ0​ and γ1\gamma_1γ1​ together bound a surface, the integrals agree”. That is false as stated, and the annulus supplies the counterexample: the circles r=1r=1r=1 and r=2r=2r=2 together bound the region between them, but if γ1\gamma_1γ1​ is r=2r=2r=2 anticlockwise and γ0\gamma_0γ0​ is r=1r=1r=1 clockwise, then ∮γ1α=2π\oint_{\gamma_1}\alpha=2\pi∮γ1​​α=2π while ∮γ0α=−2π\oint_{\gamma_0}\alpha=-2\pi∮γ0​​α=−2π. Bounding is a statement about point sets; what Stokes consumes is an oriented boundary, and a deformation supplies the orientations along with the surface. This is also why FFF may be any smooth map: a wildly self-crossing loop does not sweep out an embedded surface, and the proof above never needs one.

Example 2.28 (The annulus again, quantitatively). Take α\alphaα as in (2.13). Any loop that winds once anticlockwise about the hole can be deformed to the unit circle without leaving the annulus, so by Corollary 2.26 every one of them gives 2π2\pi2π — the circle of radius 111 and a wildly self-crossing loop alike. A loop winding mmm times gives 2πm2\pi m2πm, and the small loop γ0\gamma_0γ0​ of Figure 2.9 shrinks to a constant curve, so it gives 000. The integral sees nothing about the loop except its winding number.

The hypothesis is doing real work, and it is worth seeing where. To deform γ1\gamma_1γ1​ to a point, the family of curves would at some stage have to cross the puncture, where α\alphaα is not defined — so there is no such FFF into UUU, Corollary 2.26 does not apply, and the two answers are free to differ. They do: 2π2\pi2π against 000.

This is requirement 3 of §2.1, discharged. An action variable is attached not to a curve but to a class of curves, and Lecture 4 will produce exactly nnn such classes on an nnn-dimensional invariant torus — the basis of cycles γ1,…,γn\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_nγ1​,…,γn​ that Figure 2.12 shows for n=2n=2n=2, and Lecture 5 will integrate p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq over each of them in turn.

Summary

  • 111-forms. A covector eats a tangent vector and returns a number, with no metric involved (Definition 2.1); df\dd fdf is the model (Definition 2.2), and the picture is a stack of level surfaces that a vector pierces (Figure 2.2).
  • 222-forms and ∧\wedge∧. Antisymmetry is forced by asking that a degenerate parallelogram have zero area, not imposed (§2.2); dx∧dy\dd x\wedge\dd ydx∧dy is the signed area (Example 2.7); a wedge of 111-forms is a determinant (2.8).
  • The exterior derivative. One operator d\ddd (Definition 2.11) with d∘d=0\dd\circ\dd=0d∘d=0 (Theorem 2.13); in R3\R^{3}R3 it is grad, curl and div, and d∘d=0\dd\circ\dd=0d∘d=0 is curl⁡grad⁡=0\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad}=0curlgrad=0 and div⁡curl⁡=0\operatorname{div}\operatorname{curl}=0divcurl=0 at once.
  • Closed versus exact. Exact ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ closed always; the converse holds locally (Theorem 2.15) and fails globally. The angle form on the annulus is the whole story in one example (Proposition 2.17).
  • Stokes, and what it buys. ∫Sdα=∮∂Sα\int_S\dd\alpha=\oint_{\partial S}\alpha∫S​dα=∮∂S​α (Theorem 2.23, cited); hence the integral of a closed form around a loop is unchanged by any deformation that stays inside the region (Corollary 2.26). That corollary is what makes I=12π∮γp⋅dqI=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_\gamma p\cdot\dd qI=2π1​∮γ​p⋅dq a function of the torus rather than of the curve.
