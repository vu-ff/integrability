Lecture 2

Two languages: the Poisson bracket and differential forms

What this lecture does

Course map: G1, step 2 of 9. Two languages, each needed later. The Poisson bracket turns “these conserved quantities do not disturb one another” into a computation, and one identity, the engine, turns that computation into commuting flows: the hypothesis of the Liouville–Arnold theorem of Lecture 5. Differential forms give p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq a type. It is a 111-form, which a curve can integrate with no metric, so the action variables of Lecture 6 can be defined at all. The oscillator is the worked example.

2.1 The question

Lecture 1 left two jobs. The Liouville–Arnold theorem (Lecture 5) needs nnn conserved quantities whose flows commute, but Theorem 1.9 speaks of vector fields, not of conserved quantities. And the action variables of Lecture 6 are loop integrals of p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq, which need an integrand that a curve can integrate without a metric. So the question is

in what language is a conserved quantity written, and in what language the loop integral of p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq?

The answers are the Poisson bracket (§§2.2 and 2.3) and differential forms (§§2.4 and 2.5).

2.2 The Poisson bracket

Definition 2.1 (Poisson bracket). For smooth functions f,gf,gf,g on T∗RnT^{*}\R^{n}T∗Rn, {f,g}  =  ∑i=1n(∂f∂qi∂g∂pi−∂f∂pi∂g∂qi),so{qi,pj}=δij. \pb{f}{g}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} -\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \pb{q_i}{p_j}=\delta_{ij}. {f,g}=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂f​∂pi​∂g​−∂pi​∂f​∂qi​∂g​),so{qi​,pj​}=δij​.(2.1)

Proposition 2.2 (The Poisson algebra). The bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric {f,g}=−{g,f}\pb{f}{g}=-\pb{g}{f}{f,g}=−{g,f}, and satisfies the Jacobi identity and the Leibniz rule {f,{g,h}}+{g,{h,f}}+{h,{f,g}}=0,{fg,h}=f{g,h}+g{f,h}. \pb{f}{\pb{g}{h}}+\pb{g}{\pb{h}{f}}+\pb{h}{\pb{f}{g}}=0, \qquad \pb{fg}{h}=f\pb{g}{h}+g\pb{f}{h}. {f,{g,h}}+{g,{h,f}}+{h,{f,g}}=0,{fg,h}=f{g,h}+g{f,h}.(2.2)

Proof. Bilinearity and antisymmetry are visible in (2.1), and Leibniz is the product rule applied to each derivative. Jacobi is a line of index bookkeeping, proved in the reading that follows. It uses only that the matrix of brackets of the coordinates is constant and antisymmetric.

The bracket is what turns §1.3’s geometry back into calculation. For any observable fff, the chain rule along a trajectory together with (1.6) gives dfdt=∑i(∂f∂qiq˙i+∂f∂pip˙i)={f,H}. \frac{\dd f}{\dd t} =\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i +\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\right) =\pb{f}{H}. dtdf​=i∑​(∂qi​∂f​q˙​i​+∂pi​∂f​p˙​i​)={f,H}.(2.4)

So HHH generates time evolution. df/dt={f,H}\dd f/\dd t=\pb{f}{H}df/dt={f,H}, with the bracket in that order, is the convention of this course, and every sign that follows comes from it.

Noether, in one line

The condition {f,H}=0\pb{f}{H}=0{f,H}=0 is antisymmetric in its two arguments, so it vanishes for the pair (f,H)(f,H)(f,H) exactly when it vanishes for (H,f)(H,f)(H,f). Reading (2.4) both ways:

fff is conserved by the flow of HHH   ⟺  \iff⟺ HHH is invariant under the flow of fff.

That is Noether’s theorem, at this level of generality, and it is a one-line consequence of antisymmetry. A conserved quantity is a symmetry; they are not two facts related by a theorem but one fact read in two directions. Angular momentum is conserved because HHH is rotation-invariant, and — the same sentence — rotations are a symmetry because angular momentum is conserved.

Closedness without constancy is enough, and it is what corresponds to Jacobi in general.

2.4 Why forms: 111-forms and the cotangent space

The object we are trying to build

In Lecture 6 we shall meet the integral I  =  12π∮γp⋅dq  =  12π∮γ ∑i=1npi dqi, I \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma} p\cdot\dd q \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i\,\dd q_i , I=2π1​∮γ​p⋅dq=2π1​∮γ​ i=1∑n​pi​dqi​,(2.8) taken around a closed loop γ\gammaγ on an invariant torus, and everything in Lectures 5 and 6 depends on it being a well-defined number. Three separate things have to be true, and not one of them is obvious:

  1. The integrand must be an object that a curve can integrate, with no extra structure supplied. In particular no length, no angle, no inner product: phase space has no metric, and any construction that secretly uses one is not available to us.

  2. The answer must not change when the curve is reparametrised. A loop traversed quickly and the same loop traversed slowly must give the same number.

  3. The answer must not change when γ\gammaγ is deformed. Otherwise (2.8) labels a curve rather than a torus, and there is no “action variable” at all.

This lecture settles the first by getting the type of the integrand right: p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is a 111-form. Lecture 3 settles the second, since line integrals of 111-forms do not see the parametrisation, and proves what the third needs: the integral of a closed 111-form round a loop does not change when the loop is deformed. That p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is closed on the tori where (2.8) is taken — compact connected components of common level sets of nnn conserved quantities in involution, on which their differentials are independent — is proved in Lecture 5, and Lecture 6 then defines the actions.

2.5 222-forms, oriented area and the wedge product

What we want to measure

A 111-form measures a curve’s worth of information: one vector in, one number out. To integrate over a surface we need a machine taking two vectors, and it should return the area of the parallelogram they span — because that is what a surface integral adds up.

Start in the plane, where the answer is already known from linear algebra: the parallelogram spanned by v=(v1,v2)v=(v^1,v^2)v=(v1,v2) and w=(w1,w2)w=(w^1,w^2)w=(w1,w2) has signed area A(v,w)  =  det⁡(v1w1v2w2)  =  v1w2−v2w1. A(v,w)\;=\;\det\begin{pmatrix} v^1 & w^1\\ v^2 & w^2\end{pmatrix} \;=\; v^1w^2-v^2w^1 .   • Bilinearity. Doubling one edge doubles the area; gluing two parallelograms along an edge adds their areas.

  • A(v,v)=0A(v,v)=0A(v,v)=0. A degenerate parallelogram has no area.

  • A(w,v)=−A(v,w)A(w,v)=-A(v,w)A(w,v)=−A(v,w). The area is signed: it records not only how much area there is but which way round the two edges run.

The third property is the one students find arbitrary, so note that it is not an extra assumption: it follows from the first two. Antisymmetry is what you get for free once you ask that a degenerate parallelogram have zero area.

Antisymmetry in (v,w)(v,w)(v,w) is manifest, so α∧β\alpha\wedge\betaα∧β is a 222-form; and swapping the two forms transposes the determinant, so α∧β=−β∧α,and in particularα∧α=0. \alpha\wedge\beta=-\beta\wedge\alpha, \qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad \alpha\wedge\alpha=0 . α∧β=−β∧α,and in particularα∧α=0.(2.13) This is the same minus sign as Figure 2.4, now in the algebra. It is worth saying out loud that (2.13) is a statement about orientation, not a convention about symbols: a plane with a circulation arrow drawn on it is a different object from the same plane with the arrow reversed, and ∧\wedge∧ is the product that knows the difference.

Higher degree, and the one rule we need

The pattern of (2.12) continues. A kkk-form is a multilinear map on kkk tangent vectors that changes sign whenever two arguments are swapped, and the wedge of kkk 111-forms is the determinant of the matrix of pairings: (α1∧⋯∧αk)(v1,…,vk)  =  det⁡ ⁣[αi(vj)]i,j=1k. (\alpha^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha^{k})(v_1,\dots,v_k) \;=\;\det\!\big[\alpha^{i}(v_j)\big]_{i,j=1}^{k} . (α1∧⋯∧αk)(v1​,…,vk​)=det[αi(vj​)]i,j=1k​.(2.15) One more rule is needed, because (2.15) covers only wedges of 111-forms: the wedge of forms of any degree, which the exterior derivative of Lecture 3 uses.

Definition 2.13 (Wedge in general). The wedge product is bilinear and associative, and on products of 111-forms it simply concatenates the factors, (α1∧⋯∧αk)∧(β1∧⋯∧βℓ)=α1∧⋯∧αk∧β1∧⋯∧βℓ.(\alpha^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha^{k})\wedge (\beta^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\beta^{\ell}) =\alpha^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha^{k}\wedge\beta^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\beta^{\ell}.(α1∧⋯∧αk)∧(β1∧⋯∧βℓ)=α1∧⋯∧αk∧β1∧⋯∧βℓ. Since every form is a sum of such products of coordinate differentials, those two rules determine it. Moving one factor past another costs a sign each time, so in degrees kkk and ℓ\ellℓ η∧ξ=(−1)kℓ ξ∧η. \eta\wedge\xi=(-1)^{k\ell}\,\xi\wedge\eta . η∧ξ=(−1)kℓξ∧η.(2.16) In particular a form of odd degree wedged with itself vanishes, while one of even degree need not.

2.6 What it buys

  • A test for commuting flows that can be computed. Functions whose brackets vanish have commuting fields (Corollary 2.6), and commuting flows when the fields are complete. That is the hypothesis of Lecture 5, in a form that a calculation can settle.

  • The type of p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq. It is a 111-form (Example 2.9), so a curve can integrate it with no metric. Lecture 3 differentiates and integrates forms, and proves that the loop integral of a closed 111-form depends on the loop only through its class; Lecture 5 shows that p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is closed on the tori where it will be integrated. Lecture 3 and Lecture 4 then show that the two halves of this lecture are one structure, the symplectic form, a 222-form.

2.7 Caveats, in one place

  • Status. The Jacobi identity in Proposition 2.2 is proved in a reading; the rest of that proposition, the engine and its corollary are proved in the lectured core, except that the corollary’s last clause, commuting flows, rests on Theorem 1.9, which is cited.

  • The sign. (2.7) carries a minus sign because of the convention df/dt={f,H}\dd f/\dd t=\pb{f}{H}df/dt={f,H} of (2.4), together with (2.5). Sources that write Xfh={f,h}\hvf{f}h=\pb{f}{h}Xf​h={f,h}, or put the bracket of the evolution law the other way round, print a plus, and they make no different claim about mechanics. Corollary 2.6, the consequence that the proof of the Liouville–Arnold theorem uses, does not see the sign.

  • Coordinates. The bracket (2.1) is defined here on T∗RnT^{*}\R^{n}T∗Rn, in the coordinates (q,p)(q,p)(q,p). That the same bracket makes sense on any phase space, in any canonical chart, is Lecture 4’s.

Summary

  • The bracket. df/dt={f,H}\dd f/\dd t=\pb{f}{H}df/dt={f,H} (2.4), and a first integral is {f,H}=0\pb{f}{H}=0{f,H}=0. By antisymmetry, “fff is conserved by HHH” and “HHH is invariant under fff” are one statement: Noether in one line.
  • The engine. X{f,g}=−[Xf,Xg]\hvf{\pb{f}{g}}=-\comm{\hvf{f}}{\hvf{g}}X{f,g}​=−[Xf​,Xg​] (Theorem 2.5), checked on the oscillator’s two halves (2.6). Its consequence: commuting integrals give commuting flows (Corollary 2.6).
  • 111-forms eat vectors and need no metric. A covector is a stack of surfaces, dfp(v)\dd f_p(v)dfp​(v) counts the surfaces that vvv pierces, and p⋅dqp\cdot\dd qp⋅dq is a 111-form.
  • 222-forms measure oriented area. The wedge (2.12) builds them from 111-forms, and α∧β=−β∧α\alpha\wedge\beta=-\beta\wedge\alphaα∧β=−β∧α records orientation; it is not a convention.
