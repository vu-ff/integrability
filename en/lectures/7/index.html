Lecture 7

Lax pairs and isospectral flows

What this lecture does

Lecture 4 said what integrability is and what it buys, and nothing about how to find the integrals. This lecture gives the method that finds them for the many-body models of Part I: write the equations of motion as a matrix whose spectrum does not change. We prove that the spectrum is conserved, derive such matrices for the oscillator and the open Toda chain, recognise one in Euler’s equations for a rigid body, and say plainly what the method does not give — a debt that Lecture 8 pays.

7.1 The gap L4 left

Definition 4.6 asks for nnn independent first integrals in involution, and Theorem 4.8 turns them into tori. Neither says where the integrals come from (§4.6). The ones we own came from two sources: the energy, and symmetry — angular momentum for a central force, momentum for a translation invariant system.

That is not enough for the models this course is built around. The open Toda chain of NNN particles, H=12∑n=1Npn2+∑n=1N−1eqn−qn+1, H=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_n^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}e^{q_n-q_{n+1}}, H=21​n=1∑N​pn2​+n=1∑N−1​eqn​−qn+1​,(7.1) has two obvious integrals, HHH and the total momentum ∑npn\sum_np_n∑n​pn​, and no other evident continuous symmetry: apart from a common translation, no one-parameter family of transformations of the positions leaves HHH invariant. (The reflection qn↦−qN+1−nq_n\mapsto-q_{N+1-n}qn​↦−qN+1−n​ does, but it is discrete and gives no integral.) In the early 1970s Ford’s numerical experiments on the periodic chain suggested that it was nevertheless integrable, and in 1974 Hénon and Flaschka independently proved it (Toda, §2.10). Flaschka’s construction, done here for the open chain, is the subject of §7.6. It works by finding a matrix LLL, built from the qqq’s and ppp’s, whose eigenvalues do not move. NNN eigenvalues, NNN integrals.

Two warnings before we start. The method manufactures conserved quantities; whether they are independent and in involution is a separate question (§7.3). And there is no algorithm that finds LLL for a given HHH: Audin calls the search “cooking”.1 What §§7.4 and 7.6 show is that, with the right ansatz, the cooking can be a derivation rather than a guess.

7.2 The Lax equation and isospectrality

Definition 7.1 (Lax pair). Let HHH be a Hamiltonian on a phase space M\phaseM. A Lax pair for HHH is a pair of smooth functions L,M ⁣:M→MatN(C)L,M\colon\phase\to\mathrm{Mat}_N(\C)L,M:M→MatN​(C) such that along every solution x(t)x(t)x(t) of Hamilton’s equations, L(t)=L(x(t))L(t)=L(x(t))L(t)=L(x(t)) and M(t)=M(x(t))M(t)=M(x(t))M(t)=M(x(t)) satisfy the Lax equation dLdt=[M,L]=ML−LM. \frac{\dd L}{\dd t}=\comm{M}{L}=ML-LM . dtdL​=[M,L]=ML−LM.(7.2) LLL is the Lax matrix; NNN is its size, unrelated to dim⁡M\dim\phasedimM.

7.3 What a Lax pair does not give: the IOU

7.8 Gauge freedom

A Lax pair is not unique, and the non-uniqueness has a structure worth knowing.

7.9 The spectral parameter

The Euler top left a problem: for a body whose moments are not all equal, its Lax matrix ℓ^\hat\ellℓ^ conserves ∣ℓ∣2\abs{\ell}^{2}∣ℓ∣2 and misses HHH (Proposition 7.3(c)). Manakov’s repair (1976) is to let the Lax matrix depend on an extra complex number λ\lambdaλ, the spectral parameter.

  • A bracket. The Sklyanin bracket of Lecture 8 compares L(λ)L(\lambda)L(λ) with L(μ)L(\mu)L(μ) at two different values of the parameter, through r(λ−μ)\rmat(\lambda-\mu)r(λ−μ). Summary

  • Lax pair (Definition 7.1): L˙=[M,L]\dot L=\comm{M}{L}L˙=[M,L] along the flow. Isospectrality (Theorem 7.2): L(t)=UL(0)U−1L(t)=UL(0)U^{-1}L(t)=UL(0)U−1, so the spectrum and every tr⁡Lk\tr L^{k}trLk are conserved; at most NNN are independent.
  • The IOU (Proposition 7.3): a Lax pair always exists, its invariants need not commute, and they can miss an integral. Involution is Lecture 8’s; independence is model by model.
  • The method: choose LLL with tr⁡L2∝H\tr L^{2}\propto HtrL2∝H, then solve the linear equation [M,L]=L˙\comm{M}{L}=\dot L[M,L]=L˙ for MMM within an ansatz; MMM is only ever unique up to the commutant of LLL. Oscillator: (7.3)–(7.4).
  • Euler top: ℓ^˙=[ℓ^,ω^]\dot{\hat\ell}=\comm{\hat\ell}{\hat\omega}ℓ^˙=[ℓ^,ω^]; the spectrum sees only ∣ℓ∣2\abs{\ell}^{2}∣ℓ∣2. The two integrals give the tennis-racket theorem (Theorem 7.4) and, in the reading, Poinsot’s rolling ellipsoid.
  • Open Toda (Proposition 7.7): Flaschka’s variables (7.10), LLL symmetric tridiagonal with tr⁡L2=2H\tr L^{2}=2HtrL2=2H, MMM derived and unique among antisymmetric matrices; integrals (7.14).
  • Calogero–Moser (Proposition 7.8): Moser’s LLL, stated; Lecture 11 derives it.
  • Gauge (Proposition 7.9) changes LLL but not its spectrum. Spectral parameter (7.18): recovers the Euler top’s energy, defines the spectral curve (Lecture 10) and carries the Sklyanin bracket (Lecture 8).
