Lecture 7

When integrability fails

Course map: G1, step 7 of 9, which shows why the goal is exceptional. Lecture 5 and Lecture 6 built the integrable world: tori, actions, frequencies. This lecture perturbs it. Poincaré’s theorem says that a perturbation, under conditions stated exactly, destroys the integrals, and the KAM theorem says that most of the tori survive anyway. Between the two, resonant tori generically break into island chains and chaos. In the Hénon–Heiles system we see island chains and chaotic scatter that resemble this picture, beside the three-particle Toda ring it truncates, whose plotted orbits stay on smooth curves.

7.1 The question

Lecture 6 made the motion of an integrable system free in action–angle variables, on tori labelled by the actions. Real systems are at best close to integrable. So the question is

what happens to the tori when an integrable system is perturbed — and how do you see it?

Two theorems answer the first half, one destructive and one constructive; the section map answers the second. The worked example is Hénon–Heiles (§7.6), set beside the Toda ring that Lecture 8 and Lecture 9 will solve.

7.4 The KAM theorem

Poincaré’s theorem destroys the integrals, but not necessarily the tori. A torus is an invariant set, and it may survive even where no integral does; Proposition 7.4 cannot build it, because of the resonances. The tori to hope for are those furthest from every resonance.

7.6 Poincaré sections: Hénon–Heiles

Hénon and Heiles wanted to know whether a star in an axisymmetric galaxy has a third integral besides the energy and the angular momentum. The question reduces to two degrees of freedom, and they took as the simplest model H=12(px2+py2)+12(x2+y2)+x2y−13y3. H=\tfrac12\big(p_x^{2}+p_y^{2}\big)+\tfrac12\big(x^{2}+y^{2}\big)+x^{2}y-\tfrac13y^{3}. H=21​(px2​+py2​)+21​(x2+y2)+x2y−31​y3.(7.6) The potential has a minimum at 000 and three saddles, at (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) and (±3/2,−12)(\pm\sqrt3/2,-\tfrac12)(±3​/2,−21​), all at height 1/61/61/6; below E=1/6E=1/6E=1/6 the motion near the origin is confined to a curved triangle.4

Its potential is the isotropic oscillator plus a cubic term. Rescaled to a fixed energy, the cubic term’s strength is proportional to E\sqrt EE​ (a reading has the computation): the higher the energy, the stronger its effect.

Take the section Σ={x=0, px>0}\Sigma=\{x=0,\ p_x>0\}Σ={x=0, px​>0} at fixed EEE, with coordinates (y,py)(y,p_y)(y,py​), and plot the crossings of a dozen orbits (the tutorial at the end of this section has the code). By Proposition 7.9 the picture is a portrait of an area-preserving map, and three kinds of orbit look different:

  • an orbit on an invariant torus fills a smooth closed curve;

  • an orbit in an island chain fills several small curves, visited in turn;

  • a chaotic orbit fills a region, erratically, and its points scatter.

An island chain is what a resonant circle of a perturbed twist map generically breaks into. Every point of a circle with rotation number a/ba/ba/b is fixed by P bP^{\,b}Pb before the perturbation. After it, the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem (a reading) guarantees at least two periodic orbits, and in the generic picture there are 2kb2kb2kb periodic points for some k≥1k\ge1k≥1, alternately elliptic, ringed by small closed curves, and hyperbolic, saddles of P bP^{\,b}Pb. The two curves of points that approach a hyperbolic point, forwards and backwards in time, join into a separatrix in an integrable system; after a perturbation they generically cross, into a homoclinic tangle, which forces chaotic dynamics nearby; the reading computes one for a model twist map, the standard map. The Hénon–Heiles sections below show island chains and scatter that resemble this picture, but they are not a consequence of the theorem: the unperturbed Hénon–Heiles map has a constant rotation number and is no twist map.

7.9 What it buys

  • Integrability is exceptional, in a stated sense. Poincaré’s theorem rules out a second integral that is a power series in ε\varepsilonε, for a non-degenerate H0H_0H0​ and a dense Poincaré set; and truncating the Toda ring at cubic order turns its regular section at E=1/6E=1/6E=1/6 into a mostly chaotic one (Figure 7.5). So the integrable systems of Lecture 8 and Lecture 9 have to be found exactly as they are.

  • The tori are robust. KAM keeps most of them, so an integrable system organises the systems near it.

  • A diagnostic: a section’s curves against its scatter, and Lyapunov exponents.

7.10 Caveats, in one place

  • Status. Poincaré’s theorem, KAM and the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem (a reading) are cited. What is proved is the first-order step of Poincaré’s argument (Proposition 7.5): the leading term of any such integral is a function of H0H_0H0​, which is not yet non-existence. Proposition 7.4 is proved; Lemma 7.7 is proved in a reading, and Proposition 7.9 in a reading modulo Stokes.

  • What KAM does not say. No size for “sufficiently small”, no rate for “small”, and no integral. The Diophantine family it guarantees does not fill the resonance zones, and the theorem does not say what is there. The two theorems do not conflict: Poincaré’s rules out an integral of a specified form, and KAM supplies many invariant tori, not an integral.

  • Hénon–Heiles is outside both theorems. It perturbs the isotropic oscillator, which is degenerate, so neither Theorem 7.3 nor Theorem 7.8, as stated, applies to it. Its sections, and the regular fraction of Figure 7.4, are measurements, not predictions.

  • A positive Lyapunov exponent alone is not chaos: a hyperbolic periodic point has one. For a bounded orbit that is not periodic it is a numerical indicator, and the threshold in Figure 7.4 is a classifier.

  • Genericity, and no more. The course puts no measure on the space of all Hamiltonians. It claims Poincaré’s theorem, the effect of truncation in Figure 7.5, and, in a reading, that the integrable members of the Hénon–Heiles family are isolated, for meromorphic integrals.

7.11 Poincaré–Birkhoff, the standard map and the homoclinic tangle

On a circle with ρ=a/b\rho=a/bρ=a/b rational (in lowest terms), every point is a fixed point of P0 bP_0^{\,b}P0b​: the torus is resonant, filled with periodic orbits (Proposition 6.10). On an irrational circle every orbit is dense. Perturb: PεP_\varepsilonPε​ is still area-preserving (Proposition 7.9) and close to P0P_0P0​. By Theorem 7.8 most of the irrational circles survive as invariant curves. A circle of fixed points is not robust — nothing forces a whole circle of solutions of P bx=xP^{\,b}x=xPbx=x to persist — and what remains of it is fixed by a theorem of Poincaré.

Theorem 7.11 (Poincaré’s geometric theorem cited). An area-preserving homeomorphism of a closed annulus to itself that turns its two boundary circles in opposite directions has at least two fixed points.

(Arnold, App. 9: Poincaré announced it just before his death, and Birkhoff proved it.) Apply it between two surviving invariant curves whose rotation numbers ρ−<a/b<ρ+\rho_-<a/b<\rho_+ρ−​<a/b<ρ+​ straddle the resonance, to Pε bP_\varepsilon^{\,b}Pεb​ followed by a rotation by −2πa-2\pi a−2πa: the inner boundary turns back, the outer forward. The result is periodic orbits of period bbb that survive the break-up — for a sufficiently small perturbation of an isoenergetically non-degenerate system, at least two geometrically distinct ones (Arnold, App. 9, p. 422).

Two steps hide in “the inner boundary turns back”. Arnold’s hypothesis is pointwise: every point of the inner circle must move backwards. That holds because a circle homeomorphism with rotation number ρ−<a/b\rho_-<a/bρ−​<a/b moves every point by less than 2πa2\pi a2πa in bbb steps (if some point moved by exactly 2πa2\pi a2πa the rotation number would be a/ba/ba/b). And the two fixed points of the theorem might lie on one orbit; that there are two distinct periodic orbits coming from this resonant torus is Arnold’s corollary (p. 422): when an nnn-dimensional torus filled with closed trajectories of an isoenergetically non-degenerate system breaks up under a sufficiently small perturbation, at least 2n−12^{n-1}2n−1 closed trajectories form, counted with multiplicity, of which at least nnn are geometrically distinct.

In the generic case there are kbkbkb periodic points of each of two kinds, and they alternate round the old circle (Ott, §7.2.2): elliptic points, surrounded by small invariant curves of their own, and hyperbolic points, saddles of Pε bP_\varepsilon^{\,b}Pεb​. The resonant torus has become an island chain (Figure 7.7).

The hyperbolic points carry the chaos. A hyperbolic fixed point has a stable curve WsW^{s}Ws of points that approach it, and an unstable curve WuW^{u}Wu of points that approach it backwards in time. In an integrable system they join smoothly into a separatrix, as for the pendulum (§6.4). Perturbed, they generically cross. One crossing xxx forces infinitely many, since every image PmxP^{m}xPmx is again on both curves; the lobes between successive crossings have equal areas because the map preserves area, and as they crowd towards the fixed point they must stretch into long thin tongues. This homoclinic tangle (Figure 7.9), discovered by Poincaré, forces chaotic dynamics nearby (Ott, §§4.3, 7.2.2): a mechanism for the chaotic dynamics that Figure 7.8 shows, not a computation of that sea’s origin.

Two consequences of KAM and of the Diophantine condition. For n=2n=2n=2 it has a sharp consequence. On a three-dimensional energy level a two-dimensional torus separates, like a surface of revolution in space: an orbit starting between two surviving tori stays between them forever, and its actions stay near their initial values (Arnold, App. 8, p. 407, under the analogous isoenergetic non-degeneracy condition). For n≥3n\ge3n≥3 the tori separate nothing, and a slow drift through the gaps is possible — Arnold diffusion, which we only name.

Positive exponents without chaos. A positive exponent alone is not chaos: the hyperbolic fixed point of the standard map has one. For a bounded orbit that is not periodic, λ>0\lambda>0λ>0 is the standard numerical indicator of chaos, and that is how it is used here.

Summary

  • The problem: H=H0(I)+εH1(φ,I)H=H_0(I)+\varepsilon H_1(\varphi,I)H=H0​(I)+εH1​(φ,I) (7.1). Poincaré’s theorem (cited): for H0H_0H0​ non-degenerate and a dense Poincaré set there is no second integral that is a power series in ε\varepsilonε.
  • Small denominators: first-order perturbation theory divides by k⋅νk\cdot\freqk⋅ν (Proposition 7.4), which vanishes on the dense set of resonances; the same resonances force the leading term of any integral to depend on H0H_0H0​ alone (Proposition 7.5).
  • KAM (cited): most tori survive, deformed, with Diophantine frequencies, which are almost all (Lemma 7.7).
  • Sections: the section map preserves area (Proposition 7.9). Curves, island chains and a chaotic sea appear in Hénon–Heiles as the energy grows, while the Toda ring it truncates stays regular at the same energy (Figure 7.5). Lyapunov exponents vanish on tori.
