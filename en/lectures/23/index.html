Lecture 23

Non-equilibrium integrability: GGE, GHD, and synthesis

What this lecture does

Lecture 0 began with two experiments that refused to come to equilibrium and promised that the reason was integrability. This lecture makes the promise good. An isolated quantum system evolves unitarily, so nothing relaxes in the strict sense; what can relax is a local observable, and whether it relaxes to a thermal value depends on how much of the initial state the conserved charges remember. A generic system remembers the energy; an integrable one remembers a whole distribution of rapidities, and settles into the maximum-entropy state compatible with all the charges — the generalised Gibbs ensemble. Let that distribution vary slowly in space and it acquires a dynamics of its own, generalised hydrodynamics, at Lecture 19’s dressed velocity. Then the Newton’s cradle, finally; then the course’s dictionary, whole, with Lecture 0’s map reprinted and marked.

The hypothesis that makes a generic system thermal

Definition 23.2 (Eigenstate thermalisation cited: Deutsch 1991, Srednicki 1994). If (23.2) holds and ∣ψ0⟩\ket{\psi_0}∣ψ0​⟩ has subextensive energy fluctuations, Proposition 23.1 averages a smooth function over a narrow window and the answer is the microcanonical one: the system thermalises, in the only sense a closed quantum system allows. Nothing has been proved — ETH is a hypothesis about a Hamiltonian, checked numerically model by model, and (23.2) is exactly the statement a figure can test (Figure 23.1).

The charges are the memory

The left panel is not an accident of the observable. An integrable chain has ∼N\sim N∼N local charges Q1=H,Q2,Q3,…Q_1=H,Q_2,Q_3,\dotsQ1​=H,Q2​,Q3​,… (§13.3, Theorem 13.4), all commuting, so its eigenstates can be labelled by all of them at once. What is expected to replace (23.2) there is a generalised eigenstate thermalisation: ⟨n∣O∣n⟩\bra n\mathcal O\ket n⟨n∣O∣n⟩ a smooth function of all the charge densities cited: Caux and Essler 2013. That reduces to a function of the energy alone only if the others are fixed by it, and they are not — two eigenstates at the same energy density generically carry different Q3Q_3Q3​. The charges remember which state the system was prepared in, and a local observable can see the memory.

Remark 23.3 (What is not being claimed). Three things. An initial state that happens to carry a thermal state’s charge densities relaxes to Gibbs values, because the ensemble of §23.2 is then thermal. Some observables cannot tell the two apart even when the state can (Remark 23.9). And ETH is a theorem in neither direction: every statement here about a non-integrable chain is numerical.

23.4 Generalised hydrodynamics

Now let the state vary in space: ϑ(λ,x,t)\vartheta(\bethe,x,t)ϑ(λ,x,t), slowly on the scale of the interactions — a local generalised Gibbs ensemble. That is the assumption, and the only one; there is no gradient expansion beyond leading order, which is what Euler scale means.

Lecture 9 found that a collision permutes the momenta and leaves a displacement behind (Theorem 9.9, §9.7), and Lecture 16 found the same shift quantum mechanically. A quasiparticle crossing a gas therefore collects one displacement per particle it passes, and does not travel at its bare velocity.

Equation (23.15) has an equivalent form: the filling fraction is not conserved but advected, ∂tϑ+veff∂xϑ=0\partial_t\vartheta+v^{\mathrm{eff}}\partial_x\vartheta=0∂t​ϑ+veff∂x​ϑ=0 in the homogeneous case — not obvious, since veffv^{\mathrm{eff}}veff depends on ϑ\varthetaϑ cited: Castro-Alvaredo, Doyon and Yoshimura 2016. In Lecture 16’s units, ℏ=2m=1\hbar=2m=1ℏ=2m=1, the rapidity is the momentum: λ\betheλ obeys Newton’s second law and xxx a dressed version of the first.

One caution first: (23.17) does not say the trapped model is integrable. A position-dependent potential breaks the homogeneous charges, no Bethe ansatz solves the trapped Lieb–Liniger Hamiltonian, and (23.17) is an Euler-scale approximation, good while VVV varies slowly on the scale of the interactions cited: Doyon and Yoshimura 2017; Caux et al. 2019, App. H. It says two things.

First, at the two ends of the coupling range the trap costs nothing at all. There veff=2λv^{\mathrm{eff}}=2\betheveff=2λ — the ideal gas, and the impenetrable limit, where the gas carries the free fermions’ energy exactly (Proposition 19.7) — and (23.17) is a linear flow of period 2π/ω2\pi/\omega2π/ω: the distribution turns rigidly on ellipses of fixed x2+(2λ/ω)2x^{2}+(2\bethe/\omega)^{2}x2+(2λ/ω)2 and returns exactly to itself (Figure 23.5). That is the cradle. In between, veffv^{\mathrm{eff}}veff depends on the local state, the return is only approximate and the shape drifts — which is what the experiments see. Second, ∫ρ dx\int\rho\,\dd x∫ρdx is not conserved in a trap, since (23.17) moves λ\betheλ: “the momentum distribution does not relax” means a shape returning, not a constant.

The theory, and then the data. Caux, Doyon, Dubail, Konik and Yoshimura applied GHD to the cradle geometry in 2019, at a coupling close to the original experiment’s. The first experimental test is Schemmer, Bouchoule, Doyon and Dubail’s atom chip: one cloud after a longitudinal trap quench, where GHD works and conventional hydrodynamics, which assumes local thermal equilibrium, does not. The quantitative test is Malvania, Zhang, Le, Dubail, Rigol and Weiss, in the same laboratory’s gases, measuring rapidity distributions directly through dozens of trap oscillations at γ=c/n\gamma=c/nγ=c/n from 0.30.30.3 to 9.39.39.3 cited. There is, as of writing, no published comparison of generalised hydrodynamics with the 2006 data itself; theory and experiment meet in the later apparatus, not in the original plot.

What else spoils it. The atoms of Lecture 0’s tube are not exactly one-dimensional: virtually exciting a transverse mode and coming back leaves an effective three-body collision along the tube, which no Bethe ansatz solves, and whose rate sets a thermalisation time cited: Mazets, Schumm and Schmiedmayer 2008. Integrability is therefore approximate on two counts, and the question is one of time scales.

Remark 23.15 (Prethermalisation, and the quantum KAM question). Under weak integrability breaking a system started far from equilibrium first relaxes, on the fast scale, to the generalised ensemble of the unbroken model — a prethermal plateau — and only much later drifts to the thermal state, on a scale that grows as the breaking is reduced cited: Bertini, Essler, Groha and Robinson 2015; observed in Gring et al. 2012. This is the nearest quantum relative of Theorem 6.8, and the analogy is honest about where it stops: KAM is a theorem, with hypotheses, about which tori survive; prethermalisation is a statement about time scales with no theorem behind it. Whether approximate charges survive weak breaking in a controlled sense is the quantum KAM question, and it is open cited: Brandino, Caux and Konik 2015.

23.7 The frontier, and reduction as a construction principle

Four directions lead out of this course. Classification. Which R(λ)\Rmat(\bethe)R(λ) solve the Yang–Baxter equation? Classically there is a theorem: Belavin and Drinfeld sorted the non-degenerate solutions of (8.13) for a simple Lie algebra into rational, trigonometric and elliptic families. Quantum mechanically no list is closed, even for a two-dimensional auxiliary space and even of difference form, and Lecture 22 met an R\RmatR of no difference form at all. The systematic language is quantum groups: Yangians and affine quantum algebras, whose representations are the R\RmatR-matrices. Separation of variables. Sklyanin’s method replaces the Bethe ansatz by a change of variables to the eigenvalues of B(λ)B(\bethe)B(λ) — and §18.7 found BBB nilpotent on the homogeneous chain, so there are none to change to and the construction must twist the monodromy first. (The pseudovacuum was never the problem: Lecture 18 built it.) It is where Lecture 10’s spectral curve det⁡(z−T(λ))\det(z-\mono(\bethe))det(z−T(λ)) returns, as a quantum spectral curve. Beyond Euler. Equation (23.15) is the leading order; diffusive corrections, and the anomalous transport of the isotropic Heisenberg chain, are current work. Integrable circuits. Discrete-time quantum circuits built from solutions of Yang–Baxter are integrable models in which everything here can be recomputed, and are the form in which the subject has reached quantum simulators.

And one principle, which Lecture 12 promised would return. It built the Calogero–Moser system out of free motion on Herm(N)\Herm(N)Herm(N), a symmetry, a moment-map constraint and a gauge fixing. Reduction returns in three places, all of them beyond this course.

Quantum Hamiltonian reduction. The classical recipe has an operator version: free motion becomes the Laplacian on a group, the constraint becomes a representation-theoretic condition, and the radial part of the Laplacian is the Calogero–Sutherland operator of Lecture 21. This is why the Dunkl operators of §21.3 look like covariant derivatives: they are.

Nested systems from root systems. Lecture 22’s Aκ−1A_{\kappa-1}Aκ−1​ label is the visible end of the same structure: the Calogero–Moser models are indexed by root systems (Lecture 12, reading), and so are the nested Bethe equations (22.25), by the Cartan matrix of the same diagram.

Spectral curves. §10.5’s curve is a reduction too, of a much larger linear problem, and the modern statement — that integrable systems of this type are classified by moduli of curves with data on them — is the subject Lecture 10’s pointer was aiming at.

What all three share is the shape Lecture 12 printed: a free system, a symmetry, a constraint and a choice of gauge. It is worth carrying out of the course as a question to ask of any solvable model — what is it a reduction of?

Summary

  • Nothing relaxes; local observables do (Proposition 23.1): the time average of a local observable is its value in the initial state dephased in energy.
